Transformers & Self-Attention – Deep Dive

CSE 4137 · PDF pp. 203–248 (seq2seq and Transformer, slides by Emma Strubell after Vaswani et al. 2017) and pp. 844–867 (contextual embeddings, Q/K/V derivation, multi-head). Tags: S25 Spring 2025 · F24 · S24. The self-attention numeric appeared in S25, F24 and S24.

1. Why Transformers? From seq2seq to attention

PDF pp. 203–208 · F24: Transformers vs FFN/RNN

  • Seq2seq (pp. 203–208): input and output are both sequences (translation, summarisation). It models p(e|f) = Πi p(ei | e₁…ei−1, f), a conditional language model: an encoder RNN reads the source, a decoder RNN generates the target conditioned on that encoding. Training is end-to-end by backprop with cross-entropy (sum of −log p of each target word).
  • Problems of RNN encoders: the whole sentence is squeezed into one vector (bottleneck); information from early words fades (vanishing gradients); processing is sequential, so training cannot be parallelised.
  • Transformer (Vaswani 2017): drops recurrence. Every word looks at every other word directly through self-attention, and all positions are computed in parallel.

2. Self-attention built from scratch

PDF pp. 214–221, 845–866 · S25 S24 F24: role of self-attention, Q, K, V

Step 1 – the problem with static embeddings

Word2vec gives “bank” one vector whether it appears in “money bank grows” or “river bank flows” (slide p. 846). We want a contextual embedding: a new vector for each word that depends on its neighbours.

Step 2 – weighted sum of the sentence

Build the new vector as a weighted sum of all word vectors: ebanknew = 0.2·emoney + 0.7·ebank + 0.1·egrows (p. 849). The weights say how much each word matters to “bank” and sum to 1. Get them from similarity: a dot product ebank·ej for each word j, then softmax to turn scores into probabilities (p. 852). In matrix form, all words at once: softmax(E Eᵀ)·E.

Step 3 – add learnable parameters: Q, K, V

The plain version has nothing to learn. Give each word three roles, each through its own weight matrix (pp. 858–861):

VectorComputed asRole (analogy)
Query qq = x·WQwhat this word is looking for (the search)
Key kk = x·WKwhat this word offers to be matched against (the label)
Value vv = x·WVthe content passed on if selected (the content)

Attention probability of word i on word j: αij = softmaxj(qi·kj / √dk); new embedding zi = Σj αij·vj. Query–key dot products decide where to look; the values decide what is taken.

Step 4 – scaling

For large dk dot products have large variance, which pushes the softmax into saturation (one weight ≈ 1, tiny gradients). Dividing by √dk keeps the scores in a stable range. (Your exams ask you to divide by 4.)

Matrix form: Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax( Q Kᵀ / √dk ) · V. With input I = X + P (word embeddings + positional encoding): Q=I·WQ, K=I·WK, V=I·WV, and the layer output is Z·WO where Z = Attention(Q,K,V).

5. Multi-head attention

PDF pp. 222–226, 867

One attention pattern may capture only one kind of relation. Use h heads in parallel, each with its own WiQ, WiK, WiV of size dmodel×dk, where dk = dmodel/h (original: 512, h=8, dk=64). Then MultiHead = Concat(head₁…headh)·WO with WO of size dmodel×dmodel (slide p. 867: joining 8 heads, then a linear projection back to 512).

  • Heads can specialise (syntax, coreference such as “who → Strickland”, nearby words).
  • Cost stays similar to one big head because each head is smaller. Parameters in the attention sub-layer: 4·dmodel² (without biases).
  • Your exam asks for the single-headed layer, i.e. h=1, WO is just the final projection.

6. The encoder block

PDF pp. 210–230, 236–241

  1. Input embedding + positional encoding (p. 227): attention has no notion of order, so a position vector is added to each word embedding. Fixed sinusoidal version (p. 239): PE(pos,2i)=sin(pos/100002i/dmodel), PE(pos,2i+1)=cos(pos/100002i/dmodel). Works for any length, and relative offsets are linear functions of the encodings. Example dmodel=4: PE(0)=[0, 1, 0, 1]; PE(1)=[sin 1, cos 1, sin 0.01, cos 0.01] = [0.841, 0.540, 0.010, 1.000]. Many models use learned position embeddings instead (p. 241).
  2. Multi-head self-attention (§2, §5).
  3. Add & Norm (p. 228): LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x)). The residual connection improves gradient flow in deep stacks.
  4. Position-wise feed-forward network (p. 229): FFN(x) = max(0, xW₁+b₁)W₂+b₂ applied to every position; adds parameters and non-linearity (attention itself is only a weighted average).
  5. Stack N blocks (p. 230). Original N=6; bigger is generally better with enough data.

7. The decoder, training and tricks

PDF pp. 231–247

  • Input: the target sentence shifted right (<START> schools opened their), p. 231.
  • Masked self-attention (pp. 232–233): when predicting word t, the decoder must not see words after t, so scores for future positions are set to −∞ before softmax (their weights become 0). The encoder needs no mask because it may read the whole source sentence.
  • Cross-attention (p. 234): queries come from the decoder, keys and values from the encoder’s final outputs. This links the target to the source (it replaces the RNN encoder–decoder link).
  • Output (p. 235): after N decoder blocks, linear layer + softmax over the vocabulary.
  • Training tricks (pp. 242–245): Adam with β₁=0.9, β₂=0.98, ε=10⁻⁹ and a learning-rate warm-up (rises linearly for 4000 steps, then decays like step−0.5): lrate = dmodel−0.5·min(step−0.5, step·warmup−1.5); label smoothing: instead of the one-hot target (0,0,1,0,0) use (0.025, 0.025, 0.9, 0.025, 0.025), which penalises over-confidence.
  • Descendants (p. 247): GPT (2018) 117 M parameters, GPT-2 (2019) 1.5 B, GPT-3 (2020) 175 B trained on ~300 B tokens; BERT/prompting follow (p. 248).

8. Transformer vs RNN / feed-forward network

F24 Q1b (8 marks)

Feed-forwardRNN / LSTMTransformer
Sequence order/contextnone (fixed-size input)hidden state carries contextattention over all words + positional encoding
Long-range dependencycannot handle variable lengthfades over many steps (vanishing gradient)direct link between any two words (path length 1)
Parallelismyesno, step by stepyes, whole sequence at once
Contextual meaningnopartlyyes (e.g. river bank vs money bank)
CostlowO(n) stepsO(n²) attention, needs much data/compute
Model answer: Transformers are more effective for NLP, vision and time-series because self-attention connects every position with every other in one step, so long-range dependencies are captured without vanishing gradients; computation is parallel, which allows huge models and datasets; multi-head attention learns several relations at once; and the resulting embeddings are contextual. Feed-forward nets ignore order, RNNs are slow and forget. Costs: quadratic memory in sequence length and large data needs.

9. Exam kit

“Explain the working principle of self-attention; define Q, K, V” (6 marks) S25
Each word is projected into a query, key and value. The query of word i is compared with the keys of all words (dot product, scaled by √dk), softmax turns the scores into attention probabilities, and the contextual embedding of word i is the probability-weighted sum of the value vectors. Query = what I look for, key = what I offer for matching, value = information passed on. The weights WQ, WK, WV are learned.
“Describe the role of self-attention” (3 marks) S24
It lets every token gather information from all other tokens, producing context-dependent representations, capturing long-range dependencies and enabling parallel computation.

Traps

  • Add positional encoding before the Q/K/V projections.
  • Row-wise softmax (each word’s weights sum to 1), not column-wise.
  • Matrix order: I·W (rows are words); transposing K gives QKᵀ.
  • Scale the scores by the stated divisor; do not scale V.
  • Show intermediate matrices (I, Q, K, V, scores, A, Z, output) for method marks.
  • Masked attention is for the decoder; the encoder is unmasked.

Likely short questions

  • Why scale by √dk? Why multiple heads? Why positional encoding?
  • Encoder vs decoder; masked vs cross attention.
  • Seq2seq with RNN vs Transformer.
  • Role of residual connections, layer norm, feed-forward layer.
