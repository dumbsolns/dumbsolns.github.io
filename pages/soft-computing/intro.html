Introduction to Soft Computing – Deep Dive

CSE 4137/4237 · PDF pp. 1–40 (“Introduction to Soft Computing”). This is the big picture for the whole course: what soft computing is, how it differs from hard computing and AI, and a first look at its four components (fuzzy logic, neural networks, probabilistic reasoning, evolutionary computation). Tag: S24 Spring 2024 asked for the key principles of soft computing and how they differ from conventional computing.

1. The concept of computation

PDF pp. 2–4

  • Computing is a mapping: an antecedent (input) goes into a computing unit and produces a consequent (output), steered by a control action: y = f(x). Here f is a mapping function, also called a formal method or algorithm that solves a problem (p. 3).
  • Computing is the process or act of calculation, any activity that uses computers to manage, process and communicate information, covering both hardware and software. It is a critical, integral component of modern industrial technology. Main disciplines: computer engineering, software engineering, computer science, information systems and information technology (p. 4).

3. Hard (conventional) computing

PDF pp. 7–8, 16

  • The term hard computing was introduced by L. A. Zadeh in 1996. A computing is hard if (p. 7): (1) a precise result is guaranteed; (2) the control action is unambiguous; (3) the control action is formally defined (a mathematical model or algorithm).
  • Examples (p. 8): solving numerical problems (roots of polynomials, integration), searching and sorting, computational geometry (shortest tour in a graph, closest pair of points).
  • Premises and guiding principles (p. 16): precision, certainty and rigor. It needs a precisely stated analytical model and often a lot of computation time; many analytical models are valid only for ideal cases, but real-world problems exist in a non-ideal environment. Many contemporary problems (handwriting, speech, object and image recognition, mobile-robot coordination, forecasting, combinatorial problems) do not lend themselves to precise solutions.

4. Soft computing

PDF pp. 9–15

Zadeh’s definition (p. 9): “Soft computing is a collection of methodologies that aim to exploit the tolerance for imprecision and uncertainty to achieve tractability, robustness and low solution cost. Its principal constituents are fuzzy logic, neurocomputing and probabilistic reasoning. The role model for soft computing is the human mind.”

Characteristics (pp. 10–12)

  • Does not require any mathematical modelling of the problem; may not yield the precise solution.
  • Algorithms are adaptive: they adjust to changes in a dynamic environment.
  • Uses biologically inspired methodologies: genetics, evolution, ant behaviour, particle swarming, the human nervous system.
  • Tolerant of partial truth, uncertainty, imprecision and approximation; may give different solutions to the same input from time to time (p. 12). Sometimes called computational intelligence; it solves problems that are unsolvable or too time-consuming for current hardware (p. 11).

Four key facts (p. 13)

FactMeaning
Tolerance of imprecisionthe result obtained is not precise
Uncertaintythe algorithm may give different results every time for the same problem
Robustnesscan tackle any kind of input noise
Low solution costmakes it feasible to solve problems that would be computationally very expensive with hard computing

Advantages (p. 14)

  • No wide-ranging mathematical formulation of the problem needed; the need for explicit domain knowledge is reduced.
  • Handles multiple variables simultaneously; global optimisation strategies avoid local minima; generally cost effective, adaptive and scalable; reduces dependence on expensive traditional simulation packages (through hybridisation).

Applications (p. 15)

Image processing, data compression, fuzzy-logic control, automotive systems and manufacturing, neuro-fuzzy systems, decision-support systems, system control, prediction, and many more.

Model answer, key principles of soft computing (S24, 4 marks): soft computing exploits tolerance for imprecision, uncertainty, partial truth and approximation to obtain tractable, robust and low-cost solutions, with the human mind as a model. Principles: no exact mathematical model, adaptivity and learning, bio-inspired methods (neural nets, GA, swarms), multi-valued logic and randomness, parallel processing, acceptance of approximate answers. Conventional (hard) computing instead demands precision, certainty and rigor: a formal model, binary logic, exact data, deterministic sequential algorithms and exact results, and it struggles with noisy or ill-defined real-world problems.

Taxonomy (p. 20): soft computing splits into approximate reasoning (probabilistic models; multi-valued and fuzzy logics) and functional approximation / randomized search (neural networks; evolutionary algorithms). Soft computing is a computing model evolved to resolve non-linear issues involving unsure, imprecise and approximate solutions, wherever human-like intelligence is needed. The components are complementary, not competing, which is why they are combined in hybrids (e.g. neuro-fuzzy systems such as ANFIS, or GA-tuned networks).

8. First look: artificial neural networks

PDF pp. 23–28 · slide questions 4 and 6

  • A neural network is a network of artificial neurons, inspired by the biological network of neurons, that uses mathematical models as information-processing units to discover patterns in data too complex for humans to notice. The human brain has millions of neurons; information passes from one neuron to another, and a neural network works similarly and can perform computations faster (p. 23).
  • Biological neuron (p. 24): dendrites receive signals from neighbouring neurons; the soma accumulates the received signals; the axon transmits the signal from the soma to the axon terminals; axon terminals propagate the stimulus to neighbouring neurons.
Biological neuronArtificial neuron (p. 25, 27)
Dendrites: the input structureincoming connections receive the inputs (x with synaptic weights and a bias)
Soma: calculation processsumming junction and activation function, which makes a non-linear decision
Axon: a channel for the outputoutput connection delivers the activation signal
  • Process of NN application (p. 26): Stage 1 training: training data (input and output sets with adequate coverage) goes through the learning process, producing knowledge in the form of a set of optimised synaptic weights and biases; the network output is compared with the target, and the error signal drives the learning algorithm that modifies the weights. Stage 2 validation: unseen data from the same range as the training data is used to predict the output.
  • Types of ANN (p. 28): feed-forward, radial basis function, multilayer perceptron, convolutional, modular, recurrent, sequence-to-sequence networks.

9. First look: fuzzy logic

PDF pp. 29–34 · slide questions 3 and 7

  • “Fuzzy” means not clear, distinct or precise; blurred. Fuzzy logic is a reasoning method similar to human reasoning: it understands the vagueness of a solution and gives it a degree of vagueness practical for human decisions, widely used in AI reasoning (p. 29).
  • Boolean vs fuzzy (pp. 29–31): “Is it hot water?” Boolean: Yes/No. Fuzzy: very much (0.9), little (0.25), very less (0.1). “Is Ram honest?” Boolean: True (1) / False (0); fuzzy: extremely honest, very honest 0.80, sometimes honest 0.30, extremely dishonest 0. “Isa is 5′10″” is TRUE, but “Isa is tall” is only possibly true. Speed example: slow (0) vs fast (1) in traditional logic; slowest 0–0.25, slow 0.25–0.5, fast 0.5–0.75, fastest 0.75–1 in fuzzy logic.
  • Process (p. 32): crisp input → fuzzifier → fuzzy input set → inference (intelligence) using the rules → fuzzy output set → defuzzifier → crisp output.
  • Doctor analogy (p. 33): the doctor asks about suffering, finds the symptoms, correlates them with diseases with uncertainty and prescribes tests and medicines “fuzzily”: exactly how fuzzy logic works.

10. First look: evolutionary computation

PDF pp. 35–37 · slide question 5

  • Evolutionary computation is a family of optimisation algorithms inspired by biological evolution (e.g. the genetic algorithm), the survival of creatures, particle swarm intelligence, ant colony optimisation, artificial bee colony, or other biological processes (p. 35).
  • Loop (p. 35): initialisation → evaluation → selection → variation (crossover, mutation) → back to evaluation, until termination.
  • “How does the world select the best?” (p. 37): start with a random population, reproduce the next generation, rank the population and select the superior individuals. A genetic algorithm mimics this: a population is a set of candidate solutions, and selecting superior individuals is exploring for the optimal solution.
  • Population-based metaheuristics (p. 36): evolutionary algorithms (genetic algorithm, differential evolution, biogeography-based optimisation, evolution strategies); swarm intelligence (particle swarm, ant colony, artificial bee colony …); bio-inspired (firefly, cuckoo search, bat algorithm, bacterial foraging); physics-based (gravitational search, black hole, charged system search …).

12. Self-test (the slide’s question list, p. 40)

1. Differentiate between soft computing and hard computing.Hard: precise model, two-valued logic, deterministic, sequential, exact data and results. Soft: tolerant of imprecision/uncertainty, multi-valued logic and randomness, can evolve programs, parallel, approximate results on noisy data (table in §5).
2. How are ANN, fuzzy logic and evolutionary optimisation applied in engineering? Outline the three techniques.ANN: learns input-output mappings from data (training → weights); used for image/speech recognition, forecasting, medical diagnosis, control. Fuzzy logic: linguistic rules with membership functions (fuzzifier → rules → defuzzifier); used in controllers such as the class steam-turbine example, washing machines, automotive systems, decision support. Evolutionary optimisation: population of candidate solutions improved by selection, crossover/mutation (or swarm/ant methods); used for scheduling, routing (TSP), design and parameter tuning. They are often hybridised (neuro-fuzzy, GA-tuned networks).
3. What is fuzzy logic?A multi-valued logic modelling vague concepts by degrees of truth in [0, 1], resembling human reasoning (§9).
4. What is an artificial neural network?A network of artificial neurons (weighted inputs, summing junction, activation function) inspired by the brain that learns patterns from data by adjusting weights (§8).
5. What is evolutionary computation / optimisation?A family of bio-inspired population-based optimisation algorithms: initialise, evaluate, select, vary, repeat (§10).
6. Biological vs artificial neuron.Dendrites → inputs (incoming connections); soma → summation and activation function; axon → output connection (table in §8).
7. Boolean vs fuzzy logic.Boolean: true/false (0/1), sharp boundary. Fuzzy: any degree in [0, 1], gradual boundary, handles vagueness (e.g. “tall”).
8. AI vs soft computing?AI aims at human-level intelligence and typically needs explicit programs and exact input; soft computing accepts imprecision, evolves its own programs and copes with ambiguous, noisy data (§6).

13. Exam kit

Likely questions

  • Hard vs soft computing (table) and the principles of soft computing (S24, 4 marks).
  • Components of soft computing and the role of each; hybrids.
  • Fuzzy logic vs Boolean logic; biological vs artificial neuron; AI vs soft computing.

Traps

  • Zadeh’s principal constituents: fuzzy logic, neurocomputing and probabilistic reasoning (evolutionary computing appears in the component slide).
  • Soft computing is not sloppy: it trades exactness for tractability, robustness and low cost.
  • Soft computing gives approximate, possibly different results each run; hard computing gives guaranteed precise ones.
  • Fuzzy logic is about vagueness, not randomness; probabilistic reasoning is the component that deals with randomness/uncertainty of events.
