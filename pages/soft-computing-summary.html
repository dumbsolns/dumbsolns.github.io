1. Introduction to Soft Computing

Slides 1–40

📖 Deep dive: Introduction →

  • Computing: y = f(x) – antecedent → algorithm → consequent, under a control action.
  • Hard computing (term by L. A. Zadeh): precise, unambiguous, formal model, two-valued logic, deterministic, sequential (sorting, numerical solving).
  • Soft computing (Zadeh): tolerance of imprecision, uncertainty, partial truth and approximation for tractability, robustness and low cost; role model is the human mind.
  • Features: no exact model needed, adaptive, may give different answers per run, bio-inspired (GA, ants, swarms, neurons), parallel, handles noisy data.
  • Components: Fuzzy sets (uncertainty), Neural networks (learning), Probabilistic reasoning (reasoning under uncertainty), Evolutionary computing (search/optimization).
  • AI vs SC: AI aims at human-level intelligence (reasoning, perception, NLP) and needs programs/exact inputs; SC tolerates imprecision and evolves its own programs.
  • Brain vs computer: ~1011 neurons, parallel, fault tolerant, learns; computer serial, centralized.
  • Biological neuron: dendrites (input), soma (sums), axon (output). ANN: inputs, weights, bias, summation, activation function.
  • Fuzzy vs NN: fuzzy = human-like reasoning, simple, no learning; NN = learns from data, complex, black box.
📚 Read inSivanandam & Deepa, Principles of Soft Computing – “Introduction”; Jang, Sun & Mizutani, Neuro-Fuzzy and Soft Computing – Ch. 1 “Introduction to Neuro-Fuzzy and Soft Computing”; Haykin, Neural Networks and Learning Machines (listed in the slides’ Books slide).

2. Neural Network Basics & Shallow Networks

Slides ~41–108, 159–202

📖 Deep dive: Neural Networks & Logistic Regression →

  • AI ⊃ ML ⊃ Deep learning. Supervised learning; structured vs unstructured data. Deep learning took off because of data + compute + algorithms; training is iterative.
  • Logistic regression: z = wᵀx + b, ŷ = σ(z) = 1/(1+e⁻ᶻ). Bias shifts the decision boundary.
  • Loss per example vs cost = average over m examples. Squared error is non-convex for LR, so use cross-entropy: L = −[y log ŷ + (1−y) log(1−ŷ)].
  • Gradient descent: w := w − α ∂J/∂w, same for b. The cost is convex, so any init (usually 0) reaches the global optimum.
  • Computation graph: forward pass computes the cost; backward pass applies the chain rule: dz = a − y, dw = x·dz, db = dz.
  • Vectorization replaces loops: Z = WX + b, A = σ(Z); much faster.
  • Shallow NN: input → hidden → output; z[1]=W[1]x+b[1], a[1]=g(z[1]), then layer 2. Vectorized across examples as columns of X.
  • Why non-linear activations? Stacked linear layers collapse into one linear function.

3. Cross-Entropy, Softmax & Information

Slides ~128–158

📖 Deep dive: Cross-entropy & softmax section →

  • Logits = raw scores; softmax turns them into a probability distribution (exponent keeps values positive and amplifies the largest).
  • Entropy H(p) = −Σ p log p = average surprise/information. The negative sign makes it positive since log p ≤ 0.
  • Cross-entropy H(p,q) = −Σ p log q; with a one-hot target it is just −log of the predicted probability of the true class.
  • Binary CE (sigmoid, 1 output), Categorical CE (one-hot labels), Sparse categorical CE (integer labels). Loss = one sample; cost = whole batch.
  • Wordle example: choose the guess that maximizes expected information (entropy) about the hidden word.
📚 Read inGoodfellow et al. – Ch. 3 “Probability and Information Theory”; Ch. 6 “Deep Feedforward Networks” (cost functions, softmax output units).

4. Optimization (Gradient Descent Family)

Slides ~72–76, 599–672

📖 Deep dive: Optimizers →

  • Batch GD: exact gradient over all data; slow per step. SGD: one sample, noisy, may be unstable, sensitive to learning rate. Mini-batch: best compromise.
  • Momentum: updatet = γ·updatet−1 + η∇w, w = w − updatet; accumulates past gradients, speeds up on gentle slopes, can overshoot.
  • Nesterov (NAG): first jump along accumulated gradient, then measure the gradient there and correct.
  • Adam: momentum + adaptive per-parameter rates; defaults β₁=0.9, β₂=0.999, ε=10⁻⁸; often the best default choice.
  • Second derivative test: f′=0 & f″>0 → minimum, f″<0 → maximum, f″=0 → inconclusive.
📚 Read inGoodfellow et al. – Ch. 8 “Optimization for Training Deep Models”; Jang et al. – Ch. 6 “Derivative-Based Optimization”.

5. Generalization, Evaluation & Regularization

Slides ~559–598, 702–746

📖 Deep dive: Generalization & Regularization →

  • Pattern recognition pipeline (fish example): sensing → segmentation → feature extraction → classification. Too many or correlated features → curse of dimensionality.
  • Overfitting = great on training data, poor on unseen data; underfitting = too simple. Fix with more data, simpler model or regularization.
  • Evaluation: train/validation/test split; k-fold cross-validation rotates the validation fold and averages the score.
  • Data augmentation (flips, crops), early stopping (stop when validation loss rises).
  • L2 (weight decay): J + (λ/2m)‖w‖² shrinks weights; L1 adds |w| and gives sparsity.
  • Dropout: randomly drop nodes each iteration; reduces co-adaptation, acts like an ensemble. Inverted dropout divides by keep-prob during training so test time needs no scaling. DropConnect drops weights instead of nodes.
📚 Read inGoodfellow et al. – Ch. 5 “Machine Learning Basics” (capacity, overfitting, cross-validation); Ch. 7 “Regularization for Deep Learning”; Duda, Hart & Stork, Pattern Classification – Ch. 1 “Introduction” (fish-sorting example). Lecture slides credit S. Raschka (STAT 453).

6. Convolutional Neural Networks

Slides ~250–342

📖 Deep dive: CNN →

  • Slide a small filter/kernel over the image; dot product gives a feature map. Pipeline: Conv → ReLU → Pooling → … → Fully connected → softmax. The slides’ X/O example shows filters matching local patterns.
  • Sparse connectivity + parameter sharing → far fewer parameters than dense layers; learns the filters instead of hand-crafting edge detectors.
  • Pooling (max/avg) shrinks size and adds translation tolerance. Hyperparameters: number/size of filters, stride, padding, pool window.
  • Output size: ⌊(n + 2p − f)/s⌋ + 1. Conv params: (f×f×Cin + 1) × Cout.
  • Works for any data where nearby values are related (images, spectrograms/sound). Rule of thumb: if shuffling columns doesn’t hurt your data, CNN is useless. Only captures local spatial patterns.
📚 Read inGoodfellow et al. – Ch. 9 “Convolutional Networks” (the slides cite Goodfellow-et-al-2016 for sparse connectivity).

7. RNN, LSTM & GRU

Slides ~343–378, 673–676, 813–843

📖 Deep dive: RNN, LSTM & GRU →

  • RNN: same weights reused over time with a hidden state: st = φ(W st−1 + U xt + b). Handles sequences (one-to-many, many-to-one, many-to-many); trained with backprop through time. Variants: deep and bidirectional RNNs.
  • Problem: long-term dependencies; vanishing/exploding gradients.
  • LSTM: cell state + three gates (sigmoid 0–1): forget (what to erase), input (what to write), output (what to expose). Peephole variant also looks at the cell state.
  • GRU: simplified; reset and update gates, no separate cell state. Fewer parameters, similar performance.
  • Applications: sentiment classification, language modeling, machine translation, chat, speech recognition, image captioning (with attention).
📚 Read inGoodfellow et al. – Ch. 10 “Sequence Modeling: Recurrent and Recursive Nets” (LSTM, GRU, long-term dependencies); Jurafsky & Martin, Speech and Language Processing – “RNNs and LSTMs”.

8. Seq2Seq, Attention & Transformer

Slides ~203–248, 845–870

📖 Deep dive: Transformers & Self-Attention →

  • Seq2seq / NMT: encoder compresses the input; decoder generates the output. A single fixed vector is a bottleneck → attention lets the decoder look at all encoder states.
  • Self-attention: each token makes Query, Key, Value vectors; Attention = softmax(QKᵀ/√dk)·V. Outputs are weighted mixes of all tokens (fully parallel, unlike RNNs).
  • Multi-head attention: several attention heads in parallel, concatenated.
  • Other blocks: positional encoding (sin/cos or learned, because attention has no order), residual connections + layer norm, position-wise feed-forward layer, stacked layers.
  • Decoder: masked self-attention (cannot see future words), cross-attention to the encoder output, then softmax over vocabulary.
  • Descendants: GPT-style (decoder-only) language models.
📚 Read inVaswani et al. (2017) “Attention Is All You Need” (paper); Jurafsky & Martin – “Transformers”; Goodfellow et al. – Ch. 12 “Applications” (NLP, attention). Slides credit Emma Strubell.

9. NLP & Word Embeddings

Slides ~379–435, 845–895

📖 Deep dive: NLP, TF-IDF & Word2vec →

  • Text representations: one-hot (sparse, no similarity), Bag-of-Words (counts, loses order). Vocabulary control: stop-words, stemming (chop suffix, fast, crude) vs lemmatization (dictionary root, accurate).
  • TF-IDF: TF = count(t,d)/|d|, IDF = log(N/df), score = TF×IDF; down-weights words common to all documents.
  • Embeddings: dense low-dimensional vectors; similar words are close; cosine similarity; analogies (man:woman :: king:queen); enable transfer learning. Embedding matrix E × one-hot = column lookup.
  • Word2Vec: CBOW (context → word) and Skip-gram (word → context). Full softmax over the vocabulary is too slow → hierarchical softmax or negative sampling (pick k=5–20 noise words for small data, 2–5 for large; sample by freq0.75).
  • Skip-gram uses P(+|w,c) = σ(c·w), maximizes similarity with true context, minimizes it with negatives, trained by SGD; keeps two matrices (target W, context C), often summed. Weakness: unknown words.
📚 Read inJurafsky & Martin – “Vector Semantics and Embeddings” (TF-IDF, word2vec, cosine); Goodfellow et al. – Ch. 12 “Applications” (neural language models, word embeddings); Mikolov et al. (2013) word2vec papers.

10. Fuzzy Sets & Fuzzy Logic

Slides ~22–25, 474–540, 677–701

📖 Deep dive: Fuzzy Logic →

  • Fuzzy logic handles vagueness with degrees of truth in [0,1] (“Isa is tall” vs crisp “Isa is 5′10″”). Fuzzy ≠ probability: fuzzy is degree of membership; probability is likelihood of a crisp event.
  • Membership function μA(x) ∈ [0,1]. Common shapes: triangular, trapezoidal, Gaussian, generalized bell, sigmoidal.
  • Operations: union = max, intersection = min, complement = 1 − μ.
  • α-cut: Aα = {x : μA(x) ≥ α} – a crisp interval [a₁(α), a₂(α)]; a fuzzy set is the union of its α-cuts.
  • Extension principle: extends a crisp function to fuzzy inputs: μC(z) = max over x+y=z of min(μA(x), μB(y)). Used for fuzzy addition (e.g. “about 3” + “about 7” = “about 10”).
  • Fuzzy relations & composition: max-min composition R₁∘R₂ = maxy min(μR₁(x,y), μR₂(y,z)) (like matrix multiplication with max for sum and min for product); max-average variant averages instead of min.
📚 Read inRoss, Fuzzy Logic with Engineering Applications – Ch. 2 “Classical Sets and Fuzzy Sets”, Ch. 3 “Classical Relations and Fuzzy Relations”, Ch. 4 “Properties of Membership Functions, Fuzzification, and Defuzzification”; Jang et al. – Ch. 2 “Fuzzy Set Theory”; Sivanandam & Deepa – “Fuzzy Relations” and “Fuzzy Arithmetic”.

11. Fuzzy Inference & Control

Slides ~32, 677–701, 747–812

📖 Deep dive: Fuzzy inference & control →

  • Fuzzy system: crisp input → fuzzifier → rule base + inference → defuzzifier → crisp output.
  • Mamdani: IF x₁ is A and x₂ is B THEN y is C (fuzzy consequent). Max-min clips each consequent at the firing strength (min of antecedents); max-product scales it. Aggregate rules with max, then defuzzify (centroid).
  • Sugeno (TSK): consequent is a function, e.g. z = px + qy + r; firing strength w = min(μA(x), μB(y)); output Σ w·z / Σ w. No defuzzifier needed, efficient.
  • Design steps (steam-turbine example, temperature & pressure → throttle): identify variables → define fuzzy subsets → build membership functions → rule base → fuzzify and evaluate rules → defuzzify.
📚 Read inRoss – Ch. 5 “Logic and Fuzzy Systems” (Mamdani, defuzzification), Ch. 13 “Fuzzy Control Systems”; Jang et al. – Ch. 3 “Fuzzy Rules and Fuzzy Reasoning” and Ch. 4 “Fuzzy Inference Systems”; Sivanandam & Deepa – “Fuzzy Logic Control Systems”.

12. Neuro-Fuzzy Systems & ANFIS

Slides ~109–127, 790–812

📖 Deep dive: Neuro-fuzzy & ANFIS →

  • NN learns but is a black box; fuzzy is interpretable but cannot learn → combine them. NFS: a fuzzy controller structured as an NN and trained by backprop or GA.
  • Online (pattern) vs batch BP: update after each example vs after a full sweep (epoch).
  • ANFIS (Jang): adaptive network equivalent to a Sugeno FIS. 5 layers:
    L1 fuzzification – adaptive nodes, O = μAi(x) (premise parameters)
    L2 firing strength – fixed, wi = μAi(x)·μBi(y) (or min)
    L3 normalization – w̄i = wi/Σw
    L4 consequent – adaptive, w̄i(pix + qiy + ri)
    L5 sum – f = Σ w̄i fi
  • Hybrid learning: forward pass fixes premise params and solves consequent params by least squares; backward pass updates premise params by gradient descent. A 2-input Sugeno model with 3 MFs each has 9 rules.
📚 Read inJang, Sun & Mizutani – Ch. 12 “ANFIS: Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference Systems” (also Ch. 8 “Adaptive Networks”, Ch. 5 “Least-Squares Methods for System Identification”, Ch. 13 “Coactive Neuro-Fuzzy Modeling”).

13. Evolutionary Computation & Ant Colony Optimization

Slides ~27–29, 436–447, 543–558

📖 Deep dive: Ant Colony Optimization →

  • Evolutionary computation loop: initialize population → evaluate (fitness) → select → vary (crossover/mutation) → repeat until termination. Genetic algorithm: population = candidate solutions, survival of the fittest.
  • Population-based metaheuristics: evolutionary (GA, DE), swarm (PSO, ACO, ABC), bio-inspired (firefly, cuckoo, bat), physics-based (gravitational, harmony search).
  • ACO: ants lay pheromone and prefer stronger trails, so shorter paths get reinforced (positive feedback); evaporation prevents stagnation.
  • Transition probability: pij = τijα·ηijβ / Σ τikα·ηikβ, with η = 1/distance (visibility).
  • Update: τ ← (1−ρ)τ + Σ Δτ, Δτ = Q/Lk. TSP steps: build tours with roulette-wheel selection using cumulative probabilities → compute tour lengths → update pheromone → iterate.
📚 Read inDorigo & Stützle, Ant Colony Optimization – Ch. 2 “The ACO Metaheuristic”, Ch. 3 “Ant Colony Optimization Algorithms for the Traveling Salesman Problem”; Sivanandam & Deepa – chapters on “Genetic Algorithm”; Jang et al. – Ch. 7 “Derivative-Free Optimization”. Slides also include a TSP derivation paper (IRJMETS, Dec 2023).

14. Hidden Markov Models

Slides ~448–473

📖 Deep dive: HMM & Viterbi →

  • Markov property: next state depends only on the current state. Markov chain = states + transition matrix.
  • HMM: hidden states, observable outputs. Parameters λ = (A, B, π): transition probs A, emission probs B, initial distribution π.
  • Three problems:
    1) Evaluation – P(observations | model): forward algorithm
    2) Decoding – best hidden state sequence: Viterbi (dynamic programming, keep max-probability path and back-pointers)
    3) Learning – estimate A, B, π: Baum-Welch (EM)
  • Applications: speech recognition, POS tagging, bioinformatics, handwriting.
📚 Read inJurafsky & Martin – Appendix “Hidden Markov Models”; Rabiner (1989) “A Tutorial on Hidden Markov Models and Selected Applications in Speech Recognition” (paper).
