1. Soft computing & fuzzy logic

Key principles of soft computing vs conventional computing S24

Soft computing tolerates imprecision, uncertainty, partial truth and approximation to get tractable, robust, cheap solutions; role model is the human mind (Zadeh). It needs no exact mathematical model, is adaptive, bio-inspired (GA, ants, neurons), multivalued/stochastic and parallel. Hard computing needs a precise model, two-valued logic, exact input, is deterministic and sequential.

Define fuzzy set/fuzzy logic with example; role of membership function S24 F23 S23 F24

A fuzzy set A = {(x, μA(x))} with μA(x)∈[0,1] gives degree of belonging. Example: “Isa is tall” → μtall(170 cm)=0.6, not just true/false. The MF is the whole fuzzy set: it converts a crisp input into a degree (fuzzification), and shapes (triangular, trapezoidal, Gaussian, bell, sigmoid) set how gradual the boundary is.

Advantages of fuzzy system; neuro-fuzzy advantages; how to build NFS F22 F23

Fuzzy: human-like linguistic IF-THEN rules, handles vague input, no exact model, easy to read/modify. But it cannot learn. NN learns but is a black box. Neuro-fuzzy = learning + interpretability: the NN tunes MF parameters and rules from data; rules give transparency. Build it by structuring the controller as a layered NN (input → fuzzification → rule → defuzzification → output) and training with backprop or GA, or use ANFIS.

ANFIS: 5 layers; why hybrid is better; learning vs reasoning layers S25 F24

L1 fuzzification (adaptive): O=μAi(x) · L2 firing strength (fixed): wi=μAi(x)·μBi(y) (or min) · L3 normalization (fixed): w̄i=wi/Σw · L4 consequent (adaptive): w̄i(pix+qiy+ri) · L5 sum (fixed): f=Σw̄ifi.

Learning = adaptive layers 1 (premise params) and 4 (consequent params), trained by hybrid learning (least squares forward, gradient descent backward). Rule reasoning = layers 2, 3, 5 (AND, normalisation, aggregation). Pure ANN: needs lots of data, no prior knowledge, black box. Pure FIS: MFs tuned by hand, cannot adapt. ANFIS gets both.

Vectorized LR / vectorized 3-4-1 network F24 S23 F23 F22

LR: Z=wᵀX+b, A=σ(Z), J=−(1/m)Σ[y ln A+(1−y)ln(1−A)], dZ=A−Y, dw=(1/m)X·dZᵀ, db=(1/m)ΣdZ (X is n×m, one column per example).

3-4-1 net: Z[1]=W[1]X+b[1] (W[1] 4×3), A[1]=σ(Z[1]), Z[2]=W[2]A[1]+b[2] (W[2] 1×4), ŷ=A[2]=σ(Z[2]). Backward: dZ[2]=A[2]−Y, dW[2]=(1/m)dZ[2]A[1]ᵀ, dZ[1]=W[2]ᵀdZ[2]∘g′(Z[1]), dW[1]=(1/m)dZ[1]Xᵀ.

Why vectorize: removes explicit for-loops over examples/neurons; matrix ops use SIMD/GPU so it is orders of magnitude faster, and the code is shorter.

Why non-linear activations? Linear net = linear regression/one layer S24 F23 S23 F22

With g(z)=z: a[2]=W[2](W[1]x+b[1])+b[2]=(W[2]W[1])x+(W[2]b[1]+b[2])=W′x+b′. Any depth collapses into one linear map, so only linear boundaries can be learned (equivalent to linear regression). Non-linearity lets stacked layers represent complex functions.

“A neural network is a combination of several logistic regressions” S24 F23 S22 F24

Agree. Each neuron computes σ(wᵀx+b), exactly one logistic regression. A hidden layer runs several of them in parallel on the same input and the next layer is another logistic regression on their outputs (the 3-4-1 figure = 4 LRs feeding one LR). Difference: hidden units learn their own features.

Role of bias in LR S22 F22

z=wᵀx+b: b shifts the decision boundary away from the origin; without it the boundary must pass through x=0 and the model underfits (like the intercept in a line).

Sigmoid issues; does tanh fix them? Vanishing gradient F23 S22 F22

Sigmoid: (1) saturates for |z| large, g′→0 (max 0.25) so gradients vanish in deep nets; (2) outputs not zero-centred (0 to 1) → zig-zag updates; (3) exp is costly. tanh is zero-centred and steeper (max g′=1 vs 0.25) but still saturates, so vanishing gradient remains. Fix: ReLU/Leaky ReLU, good initialisation, LSTM in RNNs.

tanh steeper than sigmoid (S23): tanh′=1−tanh²(z) max 1 at z=0; σ′=σ(1−σ) max 0.25; also tanh(z)=2σ(2z)−1.

ReLU: definition, curve, derivative, differentiability, benefits, dying ReLU S25 F24 S24 F23

ReLU(z)=max(0,z); derivative 0 for z<0, 1 for z>0, and undefined at z=0 (kink: left slope 0, right slope 1). Still usable: z is exactly 0 almost never, so frameworks assign g′(0)=0 (or 1) — a valid subgradient. Benefits: cheap, no saturation for z>0, sparse activations, faster convergence. Dying ReLU: if z<0 for all inputs, output and gradient are 0, the neuron never updates. Remedies: Leaky ReLU max(0.01z,z) (small slope keeps gradient alive), lower learning rate, better initialisation/bias.

Cross-entropy / binary CE; softmax and its problems; computation graph F23 S23 S24 S22

BCE: L=−[y ln ŷ+(1−y)ln(1−ŷ)]: if y=1 and ŷ→0, L→∞, so confident wrong predictions are punished heavily (squared error is non-convex for LR). Multiclass: L=−Σyk ln ŷk = −ln(prob of true class); softmax gives e^{z_i}/Σe^{z_j}.

Softmax problem: the denominator sums over the whole vocabulary (e.g. 10,000 words) at every step → slow. Fix: hierarchical softmax (binary tree, O(log V)) or negative sampling. Computation graph records each operation so the forward pass gives the cost and the backward pass applies the chain rule node by node for exact gradients.

Digit recognition: LR vs shallow vs deep ANN F24

LR: simple, fast, convex, but linear boundary → poor on pixels. Shallow ANN: one hidden layer learns non-linear features, enough for clean digits, few parameters. Deep ANN: higher accuracy on complex or large data but needs more data/compute, overfits, harder to tune. Recommend: a shallow/moderate ANN (a CNN if images are large) with ReLU + softmax, regularised (dropout/early stopping).

3. Regularization & optimizers

98% train, 62% test: bias-variance, L2 vs dropout vs early stopping S25 S24

Low bias (fits training data), high variance → overfitting; the model memorised the training set. Bias-variance tradeoff: simpler models raise bias, lower variance. L2: adds (λ/2m)‖w‖² so weights shrink (weight decay), smoother function. Dropout: random nodes dropped each iteration, prevents co-adaptation, behaves like an ensemble (inverted dropout divides by keep-prob). Early stopping: stop when validation loss rises. Apply first: early stopping (free, no change to model) plus more data/augmentation; then L2 as baseline; dropout if the gap persists.

Cross-validation (S24): k-fold splits data into k parts, trains on k−1 and validates on the rest, averages scores → reliable estimate that detects overfitting and compares models.

Importance of optimizer / gradient descent F22

Training means minimising the cost; GD updates w:=w−α∇J, optimizers (momentum, RMSProp, Adam) make this faster and more stable on noisy/ill-conditioned surfaces.

SGD with momentum F24

SGD updates from one sample (noisy, slow, learning-rate sensitive). Momentum keeps velocity: vt=γvt−1+η∇w, w=w−vt (γ≈0.9). Past gradients accumulate: oscillating directions cancel, consistent directions speed up, and flat/gentle slopes no longer stall.

AdaGrad vs RMSProp vs Adam; “interim updates” variant S24 S23 F22 S25 F22

  • AdaGrad: r=r+g², w=w−η g/(√r+ε). r only grows, so the learning rate shrinks forever and learning stops early.
  • RMSProp: r=ρr+(1−ρ)g², w=w−η g/√(r+ε) (ρ≈0.9). Exponential average forgets old gradients, so the rate stays usable.
  • Adam = momentum + RMSProp: m=β₁m+(1−β₁)g (velocity), v=β₂v+(1−β₂)g² (gradient accumulation), bias-correct m̂=m/(1−β₁ᵗ), v̂=v/(1−β₂ᵗ), w=w−η m̂/(√v̂+ε); β₁=0.9, β₂=0.999, ε=10⁻⁸. Adam uses a first-moment (velocity) and second-moment (accumulation) step; RMSProp has no momentum term.

S25-f: the adaptive-rate variant with velocity and gradient accumulation is Adam (Nesterov is the “look-ahead jump then correct” momentum variant, not adaptive).

4. CNN

“Pooling always decreases parameters”? Removing pooling in a conv-only net F24 S24 S25 F22

Not always true. Pooling itself has no parameters; it shrinks the feature map, so the next layers (especially the flatten→FC layer) get fewer inputs and fewer parameters. Conv layers’ parameters (k·k·Cin+1)·Cout do not depend on image size, so pooling reduces them only indirectly. S25 misconception: pooling keeps the strongest local responses, adds translation invariance, cuts computation and overfitting. Without it the stride-1 conv stack keeps full resolution → huge FC layer, slow training, overfitting. It does lose spatial detail, but the information that matters (strongest activations) is retained, so “losing less information” is not the same as better.

Rule of thumb for CNN; normalization in CNN F24 S24 F22

Use a CNN when data has local spatial structure (neighbours related: images, spectrograms). If shuffling your columns doesn’t change usefulness, CNN is not suitable. Normalization: normalise inputs (subtract mean, divide by std, e.g. (0.5,0.5,0.5) for RGB) and use batch normalisation between conv and ReLU for stable, faster training.

Why ReLU in conv layers, softmax at output F23

ReLU adds non-linearity cheaply without saturating. Softmax converts output scores to class probabilities that sum to 1 for the CE loss.

5. RNN, LSTM, GRU

Why does an RNN forget? Which sentence is encoded worse? S25

An RNN reuses the same weight W at every step: st=φ(Wst−1+Uxt+b). Backprop through time multiplies by W and φ′ repeatedly, so gradients vanish (or explode) over long gaps and early information is overwritten. Sentence 1 is encoded less accurately: the subject “author” is separated from its verb “lives” by a long relative clause (~15 tokens), so the singular-subject information must survive many steps. In Sentence 2 the gap is ~1 token. LSTM: cell state is updated additively, Ct=ft∘Ct−1+it∘C̃t; forget gate ft=σ(Wf[ht−1,xt]+bf) keeps the memory (≈1) and input gate it decides what to write, giving a gradient path that does not shrink at each step.

LSTM structure and gates; RNN vs LSTM; peephole; deep vs bidirectional; flaws of RNN F24 S24 S23 F23 S22 F22

  • Gates (sigmoid 0–1): forget (what to erase), input (what to write), output ot (what to expose): ht=ot∘tanh(Ct).
  • RNN vs LSTM: RNN has one tanh module and hidden state only; LSTM adds a cell state and three gates → handles long-term dependencies, no vanishing gradient.
  • Peephole LSTM: gates also see the cell state ([Ct−1,ht−1,xt]).
  • Deep RNN stacks RNN layers (output of one feeds the next); bidirectional RNN runs forward and backward over the sequence and combines both, so each step sees past and future.
  • RNN flaws: vanishing/exploding gradients, short memory, sequential (slow) training. Solution: LSTM/GRU, gradient clipping.
📚 Read inGoodfellow et al. – Ch. 10 “Sequence Modeling: Recurrent and Recursive Nets”; Jurafsky & Martin – “RNNs and LSTMs”.

6. Self-attention & Transformers

Role of self-attention; Q, K, V; why Transformers beat FFN/RNN S25 S24 F24

Each word makes Query (what I look for), Key (what I offer) and Value (content I pass on). Scores QKᵀ/√dk → softmax gives attention weights → output = weighted sum of Values = a contextual embedding. Transformers beat RNNs because every token attends to every other directly (no long-range forgetting), they run in parallel (fast training), and positional encoding supplies order; feed-forward nets have no sequence/context handling.

7. NLP: TF-IDF & word embeddings

BoW problems, TF-IDF, importance of IDF, compute TF-IDF S22 F23 S24 F22

BoW problems: huge sparse vectors, loses order/meaning, treats all words equally (“the” dominates), variants of a word counted separately. Fixes: stop-words, stemming/lemmatization, n-grams, and TF-IDF.

TF(t,d)=count(t,d)/words in d, IDF(t)=log(N/df(t)), TF-IDF=TF×IDF. IDF down-weights words found in many documents (common words ≈0) and rewards rare, informative ones.

Exam recipe (football / ChatGPT text): 1) capitalise tokens, drop punctuation; 2) treat each sentence as a document, N=number of sentences; 3) build the vocabulary; 4) for each word of the bold sentence: TF = count/length of that sentence, df = sentences containing it, IDF = log(N/df); 5) multiply and list the vector.

Word embedding; cosine similarity; analogy reasoning F23 S23 S22 F22

An embedding is a dense low-dimensional vector (featurised representation: gender, royal, age, food…) where similar words are close; one-hot vectors give no similarity. Cosine cos θ=(u·v)/(‖u‖‖v‖) measures similarity of direction (instances: plain dot product, normalised dot product/cosine, Euclidean distance).

Analogy (F23 table): man→woman as king→? Gender difference eman−ewoman≈[−2,−0.01,0.01,0.08]. Find w maximizing sim(ew, eking−eman+ewoman): king−man+woman = [1.05, 0.94, 0.69, −0.06], closest to queen [0.97, 0.95, 0.69, 0.01] (royal, age and gender all match; Apple/Orange are far on the food feature). Answer: queen.

Skip-gram: steps, objective, negative sampling (k, procedure, LR form) F24 S25 S24 F23 S23 S22 F22

  • Steps: build vocabulary and one-hot input; choose a window (context words around target w); initialise matrices W (target) and C (context); for each (w, c) pair compute P(+|w,c)=σ(c·w); maximise it for true pairs; update by SGD; keep W (or W+C) as embeddings.
  • Objective (negative sampling): L=−[log σ(cpos·w)+Σi=1..k log σ(−cnegi·w)]. Raises similarity of true pairs and lowers it for noise pairs. Gradients: ∂L/∂cpos=[σ(cpos·w)−1]w, ∂L/∂cneg=σ(cneg·w)w.
  • Generating k negatives: for the positive pair (orange, juice), keep the target “orange”, sample k random words from the dictionary (probability ∝ count0.75) with label 0, e.g. (orange, king), (orange, book), (orange, the), (orange, of). k = 5–20 for a small dataset (2–5 for large) because there is little data per word.
  • LR form: P(y=1|c,t)=σ(θtᵀec) – one binary classifier per pair (1 positive, k negative) instead of a 10,000-way softmax.
  • Sentence examples (“The sun sets behind the mountains, painting the sky orange”): with window 2, target “sets” → positives (sets,sun),(sets,behind),(sets,the),(sets,mountains…); negatives = random words (sets, orange) labeled 0.

Language model, n-gram, MLM S23

n-gram: predicts next word from previous n−1 words, P(wt|wt−n+1..t−1)=count(n-gram)/count(context); simple but sparse, short context. Neural LM: embed context words, feed to a hidden layer, softmax over vocabulary, train with CE. Masked LM: hide a word (“The sun [MASK] behind…”) and predict it from both sides (Transformer encoder, softmax over vocabulary).

Roulette wheel selection; GA for TSP; Davis order crossover S23 F23 F22

Roulette: selection probability pi=fi/Σf; spin a random number in [0,1] over the cumulative probabilities; fitter parents occupy bigger slices. GA for TSP: chromosome = permutation of cities, fitness = 1/tour length, steps: initial population → fitness → selection → order-preserving crossover (no repeated cities) → swap mutation → repeat until termination.

Davis order crossover (F23): P1=1 7 5 3 4 8 2 6, P2=4 3 1 2 5 8 6 7. Choose cut positions 3–5. Child 1 copies P1 segment [5 3 4]; remaining cities in P2 order starting after the second cut (8 6 7 4 3 1 2 5 minus 5,3,4 → 8 6 7 1 2) fill positions 6,7,8,1,2:

Child 1 = 1 2 5 3 4 8 6 7  ·  Child 2 (P2 segment 1 2 5, fill from P1) = 3 4 1 2 5 8 6 7

Mutation (swap positions 2 and 7 of child 1): 1 6 5 3 4 8 2 7. For F22 (single offspring) use one of the children; with other cut points the answer differs but the method is the same.
