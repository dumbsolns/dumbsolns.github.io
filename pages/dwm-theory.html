Chapter 1: Introduction

What is data mining, why is the name a misnomer, and how does it relate to KDD?Quiz 1

Short answer. Data mining is the process of discovering interesting patterns and knowledge from large amounts of data. The name is a misnomer because we mine knowledge from data, not the data itself. KDD (knowledge discovery from data) is the whole iterative process; data mining is the step in it that applies intelligent methods to extract patterns.

Why it exists. Business, science, the Web and social media produce terabytes and petabytes. We are data rich but information poor: data sit in “data tombs” that nobody reads, so decisions fall back on intuition. Data mining turns the tombs into “golden nuggets”. Example (Google Flu Trends): aggregated flu-related searches track real flu activity and can estimate it faster than traditional systems; no single query shows it.

The name. Gold mining is not called “rock mining”; “knowledge mining from data” would be more accurate but is long. “Mining” captures finding a small set of valuable nuggets in a great deal of raw material. Synonyms: knowledge extraction, data/pattern analysis, data archaeology, data dredging.

Two views of the relationship. (1) Data mining is one step of KDD. (2) In industry and media, data mining names the whole process. The book adopts the broad view, but the seven-step KDD pipeline is what you draw in an exam.

Follow-up questions

  • Is data mining just querying or statistics? No. A query retrieves what you ask for; mining discovers patterns you did not specify. Statistics supplies models and tests, but data mining adds scalability, efficiency, machine learning and database techniques for huge, complex data.
  • Why is preprocessing needed? Real data are noisy, incomplete and inconsistent, and mining on them gives poor patterns.
  • Where does a data warehouse fit? Cleaning and integration are often done when the warehouse is built, so mining can start from clean, integrated data.

  • Can the order of selection and transformation change? Yes; with a warehouse, transformation may come before selection.

Revise: §1.2 KDD · Chapter 3 overview

Explain data characterization and data discrimination, with examples. Which functionalities should academic advisors use to describe a student group and compare it with another?S25 2a · F24 2a

Short answer. Both are forms of class/concept description. Characterization summarizes the general features of a target class. Discrimination compares the target class with one or more contrasting classes. Advisors use characterization to describe a group of students and discrimination to compare it with another group.

CharacterizationDiscrimination
QuestionWhat are the typical features of class X?How does X differ from class Y?
InputOne target class, found by a queryTarget class and contrasting classes
MethodsStatistics, plots, OLAP roll-up, attribute-oriented inductionThe same, with comparative measures
OutputCharts, curves, cubes, crosstabs, characteristic rulesThe same, plus discriminant rules

Advisor example. Target class: students with CGPA ≥ 3.5. Characterization: “90% attend over 90% of classes, study 15+ hours a week and submit on time.” Contrasting class: students on probation (CGPA < 2.0). Discrimination: “attendance 60% vs 90%; 40% submit late vs 5%.” The advisor then targets the differences: attendance, late submissions.

Book examples. Characterization: customers who spend over $5000 a year are 40–50 years old, employed, with excellent credit. Discrimination: 80% of frequent computer buyers are 20–40 years old with a university education, whereas 60% of infrequent buyers are seniors or youths with no degree.

Follow-up questions

  • Discrimination vs classification? Both separate classes. Discrimination describes the difference in summarized terms for people; classification builds a model to predict the label of new objects.
  • Characterization vs clustering? Characterization summarizes a class that is already defined; clustering discovers groups in unlabelled data.
  • Which is descriptive or predictive? Both are descriptive.
  • What else could an advisor use? Classification to predict who is at risk, clustering to find student segments, outlier analysis for unusual cases.

Revise: §1.4.1 Characterization and discrimination

Which data mining techniques should a marketing analyst use to compare the purchasing behaviour of loyal customers with occasional buyers?S24 2b

Short answer. Data discrimination (loyal vs occasional buyers) together with characterization of the loyal segment, supported by association mining of what each group buys, and classification or clustering to find and predict loyalty.

  • Discrimination: compare the groups on visits, basket value, product mix. Example: loyal buyers make 12 visits a year with a 1500 basket; occasional buyers buy only discounted items.
  • Characterization: describe the loyal segment (age, income, products).
  • Association and frequent patterns: market basket analysis: loyal customers often buy milk and bread together [support 8%, confidence 60%].
  • Clustering: find natural customer segments without labels.
  • Classification: train on past customers labelled loyal or occasional to predict which new customers will become loyal.
  • Outlier analysis: unusual purchases (very large baskets, unusual products).

Follow-up questions

  • What data do you need? Transaction data linked to customers (loyalty card), ideally from a warehouse with a customer dimension.
  • How would you judge the patterns? Support, confidence, lift, and whether they are actionable.

  • Classification vs regression? Classification predicts a categorical label; regression predicts a continuous number. “Prediction” covers both.

Revise: §1.4 Patterns

What makes a pattern interesting? Distinguish objective and subjective measures.

Short answer. A pattern is interesting if it is (1) easily understood, (2) valid on new data with some certainty, (3) potentially useful and (4) novel. Objective measures use the pattern’s statistics (support, confidence, accuracy, coverage). Subjective measures use the user’s beliefs (unexpected, actionable).

  • Objective: rule support = P(X ∪ Y), confidence = P(Y | X); for classification rules, accuracy and coverage; for understandability, pattern length.
  • Subjective: a pattern is interesting if it contradicts what the user expects, or if the user can act on it (“a large earthquake often follows a cluster of small quakes”).
  • A pattern that confirms a hypothesis the user wanted to test also counts.

Follow-up questions

  • Can a system generate all interesting patterns? This is completeness; it is unrealistic, so constraints and measures focus the search.
  • Can it generate only interesting patterns? That is an optimization problem and is still hard.
  • When are the measures applied? After mining, to rank and filter; and, more usefully, during mining to prune the search.
  • Why is confidence alone not enough? A rule can be strong yet misleading when the consequent is already common; see lift in Chapter 6.

Chapter 2: Getting to Know Your Data

What are the types of attributes? Is “monthly sales volume” interval-scaled or ratio-scaled?F24 1f · S24 1g

Short answer. Nominal, binary, ordinal and numeric (interval-scaled or ratio-scaled). “Monthly sales volume” (and “monthly income”) is ratio-scaled: it has a true zero (zero means no sales) and ratios are meaningful (80,000 is twice 40,000).

TypeMeaningExampleMeaningful statistics
NominalCategories, no orderHair colour, customer IDMode
BinaryTwo states; symmetric (gender) or asymmetric (a positive medical test)SmokerMode
OrdinalOrder, but unknown gapsSmall/medium/large, gradeMode, median
IntervalEqual units, no true zero°C, calendar datesMean, median, mode; no ratios
RatioTrue zero, ratios meaningfulKelvin, weight, income, salesAll, including ratios

Why ratio. Test: can you say “twice as much”? 20 °C is not twice as warm as 10 °C, because 0 °C is not “no temperature”. For sales or income, 0 really means none, and values cannot be negative, so ratios, means and all numeric operations apply.

Follow-up questions

  • Is a zip code numeric? No. It is nominal: numbers used as codes have no arithmetic meaning.
  • Can you take the mean of an ordinal attribute? Not meaningfully, because the gaps are unknown; use the median or mode.
  • What if sales can be negative (returns)? It is still ratio-scaled in principle (true zero); explain the net-sales case if asked.
  • Discrete vs continuous? Discrete: finite or countably infinite values (zip codes, number of items). Continuous: real numbers (height). The two classifications overlap.
  • Qualitative vs quantitative? Nominal, binary and ordinal are qualitative; interval and ratio are quantitative.

Revise: §2.1 Attribute types

Explain mean, median and mode. How do they show the skew of a distribution?

Short answer. Mean = sum / N (sensitive to outliers); median = middle value of the sorted data (robust); mode = most frequent value. Symmetric data: mean = median = mode. Positively skewed (right tail): mode < median < mean. Negatively skewed (left tail): mean < median < mode.

Positive skew. A negative skew is the mirror image: the order becomes mean, median, mode.

  • Empirical relation (unimodal, moderately skewed): mean − mode ≈ 3 × (mean − median).
  • Weighted mean Σwx/Σw; trimmed mean cuts the extremes before averaging; midrange = (max + min)/2.
  • Median for grouped data: L₁ + ((N/2 − Σfreqlower)/freqmedian) × width.
  • Data can be unimodal, bimodal or multimodal.

Follow-up questions

  • Why prefer the median for income? A few very high incomes drag the mean up; the median stays representative.
  • Which measure suits nominal data? Only the mode.

Revise: §2.2.1 Central tendency

How are quartiles, the IQR and boxplots used to find outliers? Draw a boxplot.S25 1g · S24 1a

Short answer. Sort the data, find Q1, the median and Q3; IQR = Q3 − Q1. A value is an outlier if it is ≤ Q1 − 1.5·IQR or ≥ Q3 + 1.5·IQR. A boxplot draws the five-number summary (min, Q1, median, Q3, max) with outliers plotted individually.

The box spans the IQR; whiskers stop at the most extreme points inside the fences; points beyond are plotted separately.

Worked (S25 1g): −95, −40, 2, 5, 7, 9, 12, 15, 18, 120, 255, 590. Median 10.5; Q1 = 3.5 and Q3 = 69 (median of each half); IQR = 65.5; fences −94.75 and 167.25. Outliers: −95, 255, 590.

Quartile methods differ. Your notes use the median of the two halves; the book’s Example 2.10 takes the 3rd, 6th and 9th values for 12 points (which would also flag −40 and 120 here). State your method in the answer.

Follow-up questions

  • Why 1.5 × IQR? A convention that flags points well outside the middle half of the data.
  • What is a quantile? A point splitting the sorted data into equal-sized sets; 2-quantile = median, 4-quantiles = quartiles, 100-quantiles = percentiles.
  • Variance and standard deviation? σ² = (1/N)Σ(x − x̄)² (use N − 1 for a sample); σ is its square root and measures spread around the mean.
  • Other plots? Histogram, quantile plot, q-q plot (compares two distributions), scatter plot (shows correlation).

Revise: §2.2.2 Four quartile methods compared

Explain proximity measures for each attribute type. Why normalize, and what is a metric?

Short answer. Compute the dissimilarity of each attribute type with its own formula, then (for mixed data) average. A dissimilarity matrix stores d(i, j) for all pairs, with zeros on the diagonal.

Typed(i, j)
Nominal(p − m)/p; m = number of matching attributes
Symmetric binary(r + s)/(q + r + s + t)
Asymmetric binary(r + s)/(q + r + s), ignoring 0-0 matches; similarity = Jaccard q/(q + r + s)
OrdinalRank r, z = (r − 1)/(M − 1), then a numeric distance
NumericEuclidean √Σ(Δ)²; Manhattan Σ|Δ|; Minkowski (Σ|Δ|ʰ)^(1/h); supremum max|Δ|
Sparse vectors (text)Cosine similarity x·y/(‖x‖‖y‖)
MixedΣδd/Σδ with each d scaled to [0, 1]
  • Data matrix: n objects × p attributes (two modes). Dissimilarity matrix: n × n of d(i, j), symmetric, d(i, i) = 0 (one mode); only the lower triangle is written.
  • Metric: d ≥ 0, d(i, i) = 0, symmetric, and the triangle inequality d(i, j) ≤ d(i, k) + d(k, j). Euclidean, Manhattan, Minkowski and supremum are metrics; cosine similarity is not.
  • Normalize first so an attribute with a large range (income) does not outweigh a small-range one (age).

Follow-up questions

  • Symmetric vs asymmetric binary? Symmetric: both states matter equally (gender). Asymmetric: one state is rarer and more important, so a 1-1 match says more than a 0-0 match (a positive test).
  • Cosine vs Euclidean? Cosine ignores shared zeros and vector length, so it fits long sparse term-frequency vectors.
  • Supremum vs Manhattan? Supremum takes the single largest difference; Manhattan sums them. Past paper: for (1, 2) and (3, 5), Euclidean 3.61, Manhattan 5, supremum 3.
  • Use of proximity? Clustering, outlier detection and nearest-neighbour classification.

Chapter 3: Data Preprocessing

Why is data preprocessing needed? Explain its major steps with an example.S24 1c

Short answer. Real data are dirty (inaccurate, incomplete, inconsistent), and mining dirty data gives poor patterns. The major steps are cleaning, integration, reduction and transformation (with discretization).

Quality is defined by use. Data have quality if they meet the needs of their intended use. Six dimensions: accuracy (correct values), completeness (no missing values or attributes), consistency (same codes and formats), timeliness (available when needed), believability (trusted) and interpretability (easily understood). Causes of errors: faulty instruments, entry and transmission errors, false values given by users (the default “January 1” birthday, called disguised missing data), duplicates.

CleaningFill missing values, smooth noise, remove outliers, fix inconsistenciesMissing age filled with the mean of customers in the same class
IntegrationMerge sources, resolve conflicts, remove redundancycust_id and customer_no matched; age and birth_year found redundant by correlation
ReductionSmaller representation with the same analytical resultPCA keeps 3 components for 10 correlated attributes
TransformationNormalization, aggregation, discretization, concept hierarchiesIncome 73,600 with min 12,000 and max 98,000 scales to (73,600 − 12,000)/86,000 = 0.716

Follow-up questions

  • Why does normalization matter? Distance-based methods would otherwise let annual salary outweigh age.
  • Do the categories overlap? Yes. Removing a redundant attribute is both cleaning and reduction.
  • Which step uses concept hierarchies? Transformation and discretization.

How can missing values be handled? Compare the methods.

Short answer. (1) Ignore the tuple, (2) fill manually, (3) use a global constant, (4) use the attribute mean or median, (5) use the mean or median of the same class, (6) use the most probable value (regression, Bayesian, decision tree).

  • Mean or median? Median when the distribution is skewed (income).
  • Why prefer method 6? It uses the most information from the other attributes.

Revise: §3.2.1 Missing values

What is noisy data and how is it smoothed (binning, regression, outlier analysis)?

Short answer. Noise is random error or variance in a measured variable. Binning smooths a sorted value using its neighbourhood (replace by bin mean, median or boundary); regression fits the values to a function; outlier analysis finds values outside the clusters.

Sorted price: 4, 8, 15, 21, 21, 24, 25, 28, 34 (equal-frequency bins of 3) Bin 1: 4 8 15 Bin 2: 21 21 24 Bin 3: 25 28 34 by means: 9 9 9 22 22 22 29 29 29 by medians: 8 8 8 21 21 21 28 28 28 by boundaries: 4 4 15 21 21 24 25 25 34
  • Equal-width bins have the same range; equal-frequency bins hold the same number of values. A larger bin width means stronger smoothing. Binning is local smoothing.
  • Regression: y = wx + b by least squares; the fitted values replace the noisy ones.
  • Outlier analysis: cluster the values; those outside every cluster are suspect.
In your exam, replace all values in each bin (do not leave originals).

Follow-up questions

  • Boundary smoothing rule? Each value moves to the closer of the bin’s minimum or maximum.
  • Why is binning also a discretization method? The bins become interval labels.

Revise: §3.2.2 Noisy data

What problems arise in data integration? Explain redundancy detection with the χ² test and the correlation coefficient.

Short answer. Integration must solve entity identification (matching equivalent entities across sources), duplicates, value conflicts, and redundancy. Redundant attributes are found by correlation: χ² for nominal attributes, the correlation coefficient and covariance for numeric ones.

  • Entity identification: does customer_id equal cust_number? Use metadata (meaning, type, range, null rules).
  • Value conflicts: different units, scales or encodings (metric vs imperial, grade schemes A–F vs 1–10).
  • Redundancy: an attribute that can be derived from another (annual revenue from monthly revenue).
χ² = Σ (o − e)² / e, e(i,j) = count(A=aᵢ) × count(B=bⱼ) / n, df = (rows − 1)(columns − 1) r(A,B) = Σ(aᵢ − Ā)(bᵢ − B̄) / (n σA σB), −1 ≤ r ≤ +1

χ² decision. H₀: the attributes are independent. If χ² exceeds the critical value at the chosen significance level, reject H₀: they are correlated. Book example (1500 people, gender vs reading preference): χ² = 507.93 > 10.828 (df 1, 0.001), so strongly correlated. Past paper S25 1a: χ² = 5.74 > 3.841 at 0.05, so animal type and vaccination status are dependent.

Follow-up questions

  • What does r = 0 mean? No linear correlation; r > 0 positive, r < 0 negative.
  • Covariance vs correlation? Covariance gives only the direction; correlation also gives the strength (it is scale-free). Covariance 0 does not imply independence in general.
  • Does correlation imply causation? No: hospitals and car thefts both rise with population.
  • What is the significance level? The probability of wrongly rejecting H₀ (for example 0.05).

Revise: §3.4.9 Cube aggregation

Explain SRSWOR, SRSWR, cluster sampling and stratified sampling, and how each affects representativeness.S25 1e · F24 1g

Short answer. Sampling represents N tuples by s tuples. SRSWOR draws without replacement; SRSWR replaces each drawn tuple (duplicates possible); cluster sampling draws whole groups; stratified sampling draws a random sample from each stratum in proportion to its size.

MethodEffect on representativeness
SRSWOREvery tuple has the same chance and appears once; unbiased, but a small sample can miss rare patterns
SRSWRDraws are independent, but duplicates inflate item counts and distort support and correlation
ClusterCheap (whole groups, such as disk pages), but if clusters differ from each other, whole classes can be missed: the weakest representativeness
StratifiedEach stratum is represented in proportion, so even the smallest group appears: best for skewed data

Why sample at all: the cost depends on the sample size s, not N, so it can be sublinear in N; it gives approximate aggregates (the central limit theorem guides the sample size), and s can be increased for progressive refinement.

Follow-up questions

  • When use stratified? When the data are skewed or contain small groups that must stay represented (for example age groups, customer types).
  • Impact on frequent pattern mining? A biased sample gives wrong supports; SRSWR duplicates raise counts; stratified keeps rare itemsets visible.

Revise: §3.4.8 Sampling

Explain principal component analysis (PCA): what it does, how it works, and its limits.

Short answer. PCA finds k orthonormal vectors (principal components) that capture the most variance of n-dimensional data, k ≤ n, and projects the data onto them. PC1 carries the largest variance, PC2 the next, and so on; weak components are dropped.

  1. Normalize (z-score) so no attribute dominates.
  2. Compute the covariance matrix C.
  3. Solve |C − λI| = 0: the eigenvalues λ are the variances along the components.
  4. Find the eigenvectors (the loadings), scaled to unit length.
  5. Sort by λ; keep the top k, for example enough to explain 90–95% of the total variance (λᵢ/Σλ).
  6. Project: score = normalized data · eigenvector.

Class example: hours studied vs marks, C = [[1,1],[1,1]], λ = 2 and 0, PC1 = [1/√2, 1/√2]: one component holds all the variance. For any 2 × 2 correlation matrix [[1, r],[r, 1]], λ = 1 ± r.

Follow-up questions

  • PCA vs attribute subset selection? PCA creates new attributes by combining the old; subset selection keeps a subset of the original attributes.
  • Why normalize first? Otherwise the attribute with the largest scale dominates PC1.
  • Limitations? Linear; lossy; components are hard to interpret; sensitive to scaling.
  • PCA vs DWT? PCA is better for sparse data; wavelets suit data of high dimensionality.

Revise: §3.4.3 PCA

Explain the discrete wavelet transform (DWT) and how it reduces data.S25 1c · F24 3g · S24 1d

Short answer. DWT turns a vector X into a vector of wavelet coefficients of the same length by repeatedly taking pair averages (low frequency) and half-differences (high frequency). Keeping only the strong coefficients (the rest set to 0) gives a sparse, lossy compressed form.

Output = [6.75, 4.25, 2, 2.5, 1, 1, −1, 0]: the coarse value first, then the details from coarse to fine.

  • Rules: the length must be a power of 2 (pad with zeros: a 6-value series becomes 8). Repeat until one average is left.
  • Exact reconstruction: a = avg + diff, b = avg − diff, level by level.
  • Lossy compression: set small coefficients (for example |c| < 2) to 0; the series reconstructs to [13, 13, 9, 9, 5, 5, 0, 0], within 1 of the original, with half the numbers stored.

Follow-up questions

  • Why does it help reduction? Most information sits in a few strong coefficients, so the others can be dropped; it also removes noise without smoothing out main features.
  • DWT vs DFT? For the same accuracy DWT needs less space, and its fast version is O(n).
  • Class vs book scaling? Class: average and half-difference. Book (Haar): factors of 1/√2 so the matrix is orthonormal. State which one you use.

Revise: §3.4.2 DWT

Explain attribute subset selection. What are forward, backward and combined selection, and filter, wrapper and embedded approaches?

Short answer. It finds a minimum set of attributes whose class distribution is close to that of the full set, by removing irrelevant or redundant attributes. With n attributes there are 2ⁿ subsets, so greedy search is used.

  • Stepwise forward: start empty; add the best remaining attribute each step; stop when the measure no longer improves. Example: {} → {A1} → {A1, A4} → {A1, A4, A6}.
  • Stepwise backward: start with all; remove the worst each step.
  • Combination: add the best and remove the worst at each step. Your notes explain the benefit: if A is the best single attribute, backward elimination may drop it early, while the combination keeps A and removes B.
  • Decision tree induction: attributes appearing in the tree are kept.

Tan et al. classify selection as filter (choose before the algorithm using a statistic), wrapper (score subsets by running the algorithm) and embedded (selection happens inside the algorithm, like decision trees).

Follow-up questions

  • What is attribute construction? Adding derived attributes (area from height and width) that expose relationships.
  • Why greedy? The exhaustive search over 2ⁿ subsets is infeasible; greedy choices may miss the global best but are fast.

Chapter 4: Data Warehousing and OLAP

Define a data warehouse and explain its four features.

Short answer. A data warehouse is a repository of information from multiple sources, stored under a unified schema, usually at one site, kept separate from operational databases to support decision making. Inmon’s four features: subject-oriented, integrated, time-variant, nonvolatile.

  • Subject-oriented: organized around subjects (customer, supplier, product, sales), not applications; data useless for decisions are excluded.
  • Integrated: built from heterogeneous sources (databases, flat files, online records); cleaning and integration make names, encodings and measures consistent.
  • Time-variant: holds history (for example 5–10 years); each key structure contains a time element.
  • Nonvolatile: physically separate storage, so it needs no transaction processing, recovery or concurrency control; only initial loading and read access.
Your 24/08 note says nonvolatile means “has recovery and concurrency”. The book says the opposite.

Follow-up questions

  • Update-driven vs query-driven integration? Query-driven puts wrappers on each source and translates every query at run time (slow, contends with the sources). The warehouse is update-driven: data are integrated in advance and stored for fast querying; the cost is that it is not completely current.
  • Is a warehouse just a big database? No: it differs in purpose, design, data and access pattern (next question).

What is a data cube? Explain cuboids, base and apex cuboids, the lattice, and how to count cuboids.S25 3e · 3f · S24 3b · 3g

Short answer. A data cube models data by dimensions (time, item, location) and measures (dollars sold) held in a fact table. Total cuboids = Π(Lᵢ + 1), or 2ⁿ without hierarchies.

  • Without hierarchies: n dimensions give 2ⁿ cuboids (3 dimensions → 8).

What is materialization? Describe no, full and partial materialization with examples.S24 3e

Short answer. Materialization precomputes and stores cuboids. It saves query time but costs storage. No: compute everything on the fly. Full: precompute all cuboids. Partial: precompute selected (usually frequently used) cuboids.

Example cube: time, item, location (2³ = 8 cuboids).

  • No materialization: even non-base cuboids are computed when asked: smallest storage, slowest queries.
  • Full: store all 8: fastest queries, but storage grows exponentially with dimensions and levels (Π(Lᵢ + 1) cuboids).
  • Partial: store, for example, the base cuboid, (time, item), (item) and the apex; answer other queries from the nearest stored cuboid. This is the practical choice.

Choose cuboids by workload, access frequency, cost, update cost and storage. An iceberg cube stores only cells whose aggregate passes a threshold; a shell cube precomputes cuboids on only a few dimensions.

Follow-up questions

  • Which materialized cuboid answers a query? One with the needed dimensions at the same or finer level; the cheapest by estimated cost.
  • Can a country-level cuboid answer a state-level query? No: finer data cannot be rebuilt from coarser data.

Explain data cube measures and classify them by aggregation behaviour.F24 3f

Short answer. A measure is a numeric value (such as dollars sold) aggregated for each cell. Distributive: partition, aggregate each part, then aggregate the results gives the same answer (sum, count, min, max). Algebraic: computed from a fixed number of distributive measures (average = sum/count). Holistic: no fixed-size summary is enough (median, mode, rank).

  • Distributive example: annual sales = sum of the four quarterly sums, so roll-up is easy.
  • Algebraic example: avg stores sum and count per cell; standard deviation from count, sum and sum of squares. Averaging stored averages is wrong unless counts are carried.
  • Holistic example: the yearly median needs all quarterly values; it cannot be built from sub-cuboid medians. Approximations exist.

Chapter 6: Frequent Patterns, Associations and Correlations

Define support, confidence, frequent itemset, association rule and strong rule.

Short answer. Support(A ⇒ B) = P(A ∪ B) = count(A ∪ B)/|D|. Confidence(A ⇒ B) = P(B | A) = count(A ∪ B)/count(A). An itemset is frequent if its support meets min_sup. A rule is strong if it meets both min_sup and min_conf.

  • Itemset: a set of items; a k-itemset has k items. A transaction contains A if A ⊆ T.
  • Support count (absolute support) is the number of transactions containing the itemset; relative support divides by |D|.
  • P(A ∪ B) means a transaction contains all items of A and B, not “A or B”.
  • Association rule: A ⇒ B with A, B non-empty and A ∩ B = ∅. Example: computer ⇒ antivirus software [support 2%, confidence 60%]: 2% of all transactions contain both; 60% of computer buyers also bought the software.
  • Two-step process: (1) find all frequent itemsets (the expensive step), (2) generate strong rules from them (cheap).

Follow-up questions

  • Why is support usually small? There are many transactions, and it is the share containing the whole rule (your notes). Confidence behaves like a conditional probability.
  • Which count is the denominator of confidence? The count of the left side.
  • Single-dimensional vs multidimensional rules? One repeated predicate (buys) vs several (age, income, buys).

Revise: §6.1 Basics

State the Apriori property and explain the Apriori algorithm and its limitations.

Short answer. Apriori property: every non-empty subset of a frequent itemset must also be frequent. The algorithm searches level by level: L₁ from one scan, then Ck by joining Lk−1 with itself, prune candidates with an infrequent (k−1)-subset, count, keep Lk; stop when Lk is empty.

  • Why it works: adding an item cannot raise support, so if I fails min_sup, I ∪ {A} fails too (antimonotone).
  • Join: two sorted itemsets join if their first k−2 items are equal and the last items differ (each candidate once). Prune: remove candidates with an infrequent (k−1)-subset before counting.
  • Example (Table 6.1, min_sup 2): 13 frequent itemsets; of six joined 3-candidates only two were counted.

Limitations: (1) a huge number of candidates (10⁴ frequent items give over 10⁷ candidate pairs); (2) one database scan per level; (3) heavy work for long patterns.

Follow-up questions

  • Is Apriori a rule generator? No: it finds frequent itemsets; rules are a separate step.
  • How many scans? One per level that produces candidates.
  • Tan’s variants? Candidates by Fk−1 × F₁ or Fk−1 × Fk−1 merging, with support counted using a hash tree.

Compare FP-growth with Apriori. Describe the FP-tree method and its advantages and limits.

Short answer. FP-growth compresses the database into a frequent pattern tree after two scans and mines it by pattern growth, with no candidate generation. It finds the same itemsets as Apriori and is reported to be about an order of magnitude faster.

  1. Scan 1: count items, keep those ≥ min_sup, order by descending count.
  2. Scan 2: sort each transaction’s frequent items in that order and insert into a tree under a null root; shared prefixes only raise counts.
  3. For each item, from the least frequent upward, build its conditional pattern base (prefix paths with counts) and conditional FP-tree (items whose total ≥ min_sup).
  4. Combine the suffix item with every pattern from its conditional tree.

  • Why start from the least frequent item? Its prefix paths are short and selective, and each pattern is found exactly once.

  • Single-path shortcut? If a conditional tree is a single path, every combination of its nodes is a pattern.

Revise: §6.2.4 FP-growth · FP-growth how-to and calculator

How can the efficiency of Apriori be improved?

Short answer. Hash-based counting, transaction reduction, partitioning, sampling and dynamic itemset counting.

  • Hash-based counting: while counting 1-itemsets, hash every 2-itemset of each transaction into buckets; a pair in a bucket whose count is below min_sup cannot be frequent. Most useful at k = 2.
  • Transaction reduction: a transaction with no frequent k-itemset cannot contain a frequent (k+1)-itemset, so drop it from later scans.
  • Partitioning (two scans): split the data into memory-sized partitions; find local frequent itemsets in each; one more scan counts the union. Any globally frequent itemset is locally frequent in at least one partition, since otherwise its total would be below min_sup × |D|.
  • Sampling: mine a random sample in memory with a lowered threshold, then verify on the rest; trades some accuracy for speed.
  • Dynamic itemset counting: new candidates can start counting at block boundaries, which needs fewer scans.

Follow-up questions

  • Why does partitioning need only two scans? Phase I is done in memory per partition; phase II is the single global count.

What is the vertical data format (Eclat), and what is a diffset?

Short answer. Horizontal format stores {TID : itemset}; vertical format stores {item : TID set}. The support of an itemset is the size of its TID set, and a (k+1)-itemset’s TID set is the intersection of two k-itemset TID sets, so no more database scans are needed after the first conversion.

I1 {T100,T400,T500,T700,T800,T900} I2 {T100,T200,T300,T400,T600,T800,T900} I1 ∩ I2 = {T100,T400,T800,T900} → support 4 I1I2 ∩ I1I3 gives the TID set of I1I2I3 = {T800,T900} → support 2
  • Drawback: TID sets can be very long. A diffset stores only the difference: diffset({I1,I2},{I1}) = {T500, T700}, so support({I1,I2}) = 6 − 2 = 4. It helps most on dense data.

Why are support and confidence not enough? Explain lift, χ² and the null-invariant measures.

Short answer. A strong rule can mislead because confidence ignores the base rate of the consequent. Lift = P(A ∪ B)/(P(A)P(B)) tests correlation: > 1 positive, = 1 independent, < 1 negative. χ² also tests correlation. For data with many null transactions use null-invariant measures (all_conf, max_conf, Kulczynski, cosine), preferably Kulczynski with the imbalance ratio.

Book example: of 10,000 transactions, 6000 contain games, 7500 videos, 4000 both. game ⇒ video has support 40% and confidence 66%, so it is strong, yet P(video) = 75%, so buying games lowers the chance of buying videos. Lift = 0.40/(0.60 × 0.75) = 0.89 < 1. χ² = 555.6, with the observed count 4000 below the expected 4500: negative correlation.

all_conf = sup(A∪B) / max(sup A, sup B) max_conf = max(P(A|B), P(B|A)) Kulc = ½ (P(A|B) + P(B|A)) cosine = sup(A∪B) / √(sup A × sup B) IR = |sup A − sup B| / (sup A + sup B − sup(A∪B)) (0 = balanced, near 1 = skewed)
  • Null-transaction: contains none of the itemsets examined. A measure is null-invariant if null-transactions do not change it. Lift and χ² are not; the four above are.
  • In the book’s milk–coffee table, two datasets differing only in the null count give lift 9.26 vs 1 and χ² 90,557 vs 0, while all four null-invariant measures stay at 0.91.

Follow-up questions

  • Why add IR to Kulc? Kulc alone gives 0.5 for both balanced and very skewed pairs; IR shows the imbalance.
  • Other measures? Leverage = P(AB) − P(A)P(B); conviction = (1 − sup B)/(1 − conf). Simpson’s paradox: a correlation can reverse when the data are split into subgroups.

Revise: §6.3 Lift and χ² · Kulc and IR

How are association rules generated from frequent itemsets? How can the work be reduced?

Short answer. For each frequent itemset l and each non-empty proper subset s, output s ⇒ (l − s) if count(l)/count(s) ≥ min_conf. A k-itemset gives 2ᵏ − 2 candidate rules. All rules from one itemset share the same support but differ in confidence.

l = {Pen, Marker, Ruler}, count 2 (S25 1d, min_conf 50%) Pen ⇒ Marker,Ruler 2/4 = 50% yes Marker ⇒ Pen,Ruler 2/3 = 66.7% yes Ruler ⇒ Pen,Marker 2/2 = 100% yes Pen,Marker ⇒ Ruler 2/2 = 100% yes Pen,Ruler ⇒ Marker 2/2 = 100% yes Marker,Ruler ⇒ Pen 2/2 = 100% yes

Reducing work: confidence(s′ ⇒ l − s′) ≤ confidence(s ⇒ l − s) for s′ ⊂ s, since count(s′) ≥ count(s). So if a rule fails, every rule with a smaller left side from the same itemset also fails: start with the largest left sides and prune downward.

Follow-up questions

  • Do rules need a separate support check? No: the itemset is already frequent.
  • Can the right side have several items? Yes.
  • If the question fixes “A on the left, B on the right”? Keep only rules that satisfy it; other items can be on either side.

Cross-chapter questions

How do data warehousing, preprocessing and mining fit together in one project?

Short answer. The warehouse supplies cleaned, integrated, historical data; OLAP explores it; mining then discovers patterns. Cleaning and integration (KDD steps 1–2) are done while loading the warehouse.

sources ─ ETL (cleaning, integration, transformation) ─▶ warehouse / data cube ─▶ OLAP exploration (roll-up, drill-down, slice, dice) ─▶ select task-relevant data ─▶ further preprocessing (reduction, normalization) ─▶ data mining ─▶ pattern evaluation (support, confidence, lift) ─▶ presentation

Follow-up questions

  • What is OLAM? Mining combined with OLAP, so patterns can be mined at several abstraction levels.
  • Why does a warehouse help mining? The data are clean, integrated and summarized, so preprocessing is already done.
