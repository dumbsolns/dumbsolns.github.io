1.1 Why data mining?

1.1.1 The data age: data rich but information poor

We live in the data age: terabytes and petabytes arrive daily from business (a large chain handles hundreds of millions of transactions a week), science and engineering, medicine, the Web, and social media. This growth comes from the computerization of society and powerful data-collection and storage tools.

The result is a data rich but information poor situation. Data become “data tombs”, archives seldom visited, so decisions rely on intuition. Expert systems (knowledge entered by hand) are costly, slow and biased. Data mining turns data tombs into “golden nuggets” of knowledge.

Example 1.1 (Google Flu Trends): aggregated flu-related searches track real flu activity and can estimate it up to two weeks faster than traditional systems. A pattern appears only when the queries are aggregated; no single query shows it.

Slides add a view of the evolution of science: empirical (before 1600), theoretical (1600–1950s), computational (1950s–1990s), and data science (1990 to now), where data mining is a major challenge.

1.2 What is data mining?

Definition, the “misnomer”, and the KDD process

Data mining is the process of discovering interesting patterns and knowledge from large amounts of data. The sources can be databases, data warehouses, the Web, other repositories, or streamed data. Your notes: extracting important and interesting information or patterns from any data set.

The name is a misnomer: gold mining is not called “rock mining”, so “knowledge mining from data” would be more accurate, but it is long. “Mining” captures finding a small set of precious nuggets in a great deal of raw material. Similar terms: knowledge extraction, data/pattern analysis, data archaeology, data dredging.

KDD (knowledge discovery from data) is an iterative sequence of seven steps (Figure 1.4):

#StepPurpose
1Data cleaningRemove noise and inconsistent data
2Data integrationCombine multiple data sources
3Data selectionRetrieve data relevant to the analysis task
4Data transformationConsolidate into forms for mining, by summary or aggregation operations
5Data miningApply intelligent methods to extract patterns (an essential step)
6Pattern evaluationIdentify truly interesting patterns using interestingness measures
7Knowledge presentationVisualization and knowledge representation for users
  • Steps 1–4 are data preprocessing. The data mining step may interact with the user or a knowledge base, and interesting patterns can be stored as new knowledge.
  • Two views: data mining as one step of KDD, or (common in industry and media) as the whole process. The book adopts the broad view.
  • Footnotes: cleaning and integration are often done as preprocessing whose output is stored in a data warehouse; transformation may precede selection (especially with warehouses); data reduction may also be applied.
Your notes list the steps as “sequential”. The book calls the process iterative (you may loop back to earlier steps). Mention both if a question asks: “steps in order, with feedback loops”.

1.3 What kinds of data can be mined?

1.3.1 Database data (relational databases)

A DBMS is a collection of interrelated data (the database) plus programs to define structures, manage concurrent, shared or distributed access, and ensure consistency and security. A relational database is a collection of tables with unique names; each table has attributes (columns, fields) and tuples (records, rows). Each tuple is an object with a unique key. An ER model describes entities and relationships.

Example 1.2 (AllElectronics schema):

customer (cust_ID, name, address, age, occupation, annual_income, credit_information, category, …) item (item_ID, brand, category, type, price, place_made, supplier, cost, …) employee (empl_ID, name, category, group, salary, commission, …) branch (branch_ID, name, address, …) purchases (trans_ID, cust_ID, empl_ID, date, time, method_paid, amount) items_sold(trans_ID, item_ID, qty) works_at(empl_ID, branch_ID)

Queries (SQL) retrieve subsets and use aggregates (sum, avg, count, max, min): “total sales of last month grouped by branch”. Mining goes further: predict the credit risk of new customers from income, age and credit history, or detect deviations (items whose sales differ greatly from last year), which may point to a packaging or price change.

Han et al., §1.3.1 “Database Data”, Example 1.2, Figure 1.5.

1.3.2 Data warehouses, data cubes and OLAP

A data warehouse is a repository of information from multiple sources, stored under a unified schema, usually at a single site. It is built by data cleaning, integration, transformation, loading and periodic refreshing. Data are organized around subjects (customer, item, supplier, activity), stored from a historical perspective (for example the past 6 to 12 months), and typically summarized (sales per item type per store or region rather than each transaction).

A warehouse is modelled by a data cube: each dimension is an attribute or set of attributes, and each cell holds an aggregate measure (count, sum of sales). Example 1.3: dimensions address (Chicago, New York, Toronto, Vancouver), time (Q1–Q4) and item (home entertainment, computer, phone, security); the cell ⟨Vancouver, Q1, security⟩ holds $400,000.

  • OLAP operations present data at different levels of abstraction: drill-down (quarter to month) and roll-up (city to country).
  • Multidimensional data mining (exploratory) does data mining in OLAP style: patterns across combinations of dimensions at varying granularity.
  • Past-paper link: why a separate warehouse, and warehouse vs database (Chapter 4).

Han et al., §1.3.2 “Data Warehouses”, Example 1.3, Figures 1.6–1.7; Chapter 4 deep dive. Notes: “Data Warehouse: NOT Database” page.

1.3.3–1.3.4 Transactional data and other kinds of data

Transactional data: each record is one transaction (a purchase, a flight booking, a user’s clicks) with a trans_ID and a list of items. Example 1.4, Figure 1.8: T100 {I1, I3, I8, I16}, T200 {I2, I8}. It is a nested relation (the item list is a set), so it is stored as a flat file or unfolded into an items_sold table. A plain database system cannot answer market basket questions (“which items sell together?”), but mining frequent itemsets can (Chapter 6). Use: bundle printers with computers, or discount one to sell the other.

Other kinds: time-related or sequence data (stock, bio-sequences), data streams (sensors, surveillance), spatial data (maps), engineering design data, hypertext and multimedia (text, image, video, audio), graph and networked data (social networks), and the Web. Each adds structure and semantics (order, connectivity) and new challenges. Examples: schedule bank tellers by traffic trends; detect network intrusions from stream anomalies; mine product comments for customer sentiment; detect goals in hockey video. Often several types coexist (Web pages mix text, images and links).

Han et al., §1.3.3 “Transactional Data”, Example 1.4, §1.3.4 “Other Kinds of Data”. Notes: 24/06/26 (frequent itemset = same transaction; frequent subsequence = different transactions).

1.4 What kinds of patterns can be mined?

Overview: descriptive vs predictive

Functionalities specify the kinds of patterns to find. Tasks are descriptive (characterize general properties of the target data) or predictive (induce from current data to make predictions).

1.4.1 Class/concept description: characterization and discrimination

Data are associated with classes (item classes: computers, printers) or concepts (customer concepts: bigSpenders, budgetSpenders). A class/concept description summarizes a class concisely and precisely, by characterization, discrimination, or both.

  • Characterization: summarizes the general features of the target class, usually collected by a query. Methods: simple statistics and plots (Chapter 2), OLAP roll-up, and attribute-oriented induction (Chapter 4). Output: pie and bar charts, curves, cubes, crosstabs, generalized relations, or characteristic rules. Example 1.5: customers who spend more than $5000 a year are 40 to 50 years old, employed, with excellent credit.
  • Discrimination: compares the target class with one or more contrasting classes; output includes comparative measures; in rule form, discriminant rules. Example 1.6: 80% of customers who frequently buy computer products are 20 to 40 years old with a university education, whereas 60% of those who rarely buy them are seniors or youths with no degree.

Han et al., §1.4.1 “Class/Concept Description: Characterization and Discrimination”, Examples 1.5–1.6. Notes: 24/06/26.

1.4.2 Frequent patterns, associations and correlations

Frequent patterns occur frequently in data. Kinds: frequent itemsets (milk and bread bought together), frequent subsequences or sequential patterns (laptop, then digital camera, then memory card), and frequent substructures (graphs, trees, lattices that occur often). They lead to associations and correlations.

Example 1.7: buys(X, “computer”) ⇒ buys(X, “software”) [support = 1%, confidence = 50%], written simply computer ⇒ software [1%, 50%]. Support 1%: 1% of all transactions contain both. Confidence 50%: if a customer buys a computer there is a 50% chance of buying software.

  • Single-dimensional association rule: one repeated predicate (buys).
  • Multidimensional association rule: several predicates: age(X, “20..29”) ∧ income(X, “40K..49K”) ⇒ buys(X, “laptop”) [2%, 60%].
  • Rules are discarded as uninteresting unless they meet both minimum support and minimum confidence. Statistical correlation analysis can then refine them (Chapter 6).

Your notes: support is the percentage of all transactions containing the rule, so it is usually small when there are many transactions; confidence is like a conditional probability.

Han et al., §1.4.2 “Mining Frequent Patterns, Associations, and Correlations”, Example 1.7; Chapter 6 deep dive. Notes: 24/06/26.

1.4.3 Classification and regression

Classification finds a model (function) that describes and distinguishes data classes, derived from training data (objects whose class labels are known), then used to predict the label of objects whose label is unknown. Notes: training data have the class-attribute value; test data do not.

  • Forms of the model: IF-THEN classification rules; a decision tree (nodes test an attribute, branches are outcomes, leaves are classes); mathematical formulae; neural networks (weighted-connection units). Others: naive Bayes, support vector machines, k-nearest-neighbour.
  • Regression models continuous-valued functions, predicting a missing or unavailable numeric value. Prediction covers both numeric prediction and class-label prediction.
  • Relevance analysis may come first: pick the attributes that matter and drop the irrelevant ones.

Example 1.8: classify items by campaign response (good, mild, none) with a decision tree showing price as the best discriminator (then brand, place made); predicting each item’s revenue during a sale is regression.

Han et al., §1.4.3 “Classification and Regression for Predictive Analysis”, Example 1.8, Figure 1.9. Notes: 25/06/26 (class attribute, training and testing).

1.4.4–1.4.5 Cluster analysis and outlier analysis

Clustering analyzes objects without class labels (often none exist at the start). Principle: maximize intraclass similarity and minimize interclass similarity, so objects in a cluster resemble each other and differ from other clusters. Each cluster can be seen as a class and used to derive rules; it also supports taxonomy formation (a hierarchy of classes). Example 1.9: customer locations in a city form three clusters, giving target groups for marketing.

Outliers are objects that do not comply with the general behaviour or model of the data. Many methods discard them as noise, but in fraud detection the rare events are the interesting ones. The analysis is outlier analysis or anomaly mining.

  • Statistical tests assume a distribution or probability model.
  • Distance measures: objects remote from any cluster are outliers.
  • Density-based methods: find outliers in a local region that look normal globally.

Example 1.10: unusually large purchases for an account, or unusual locations, types or frequency of purchases, suggest credit card fraud.

Han et al., §1.4.4 “Cluster Analysis”, §1.4.5 “Outlier Analysis”, Examples 1.9–1.10, Figure 1.10. Notes: 25/06/26 (outliers are extreme values).

1.4.6 Are all patterns interesting?

A system can generate thousands or millions of patterns, and only a small fraction interest a user. A pattern is interesting if it is:

  1. Easily understood by humans,
  2. Valid on new or test data with some degree of certainty,
  3. Potentially useful, and
  4. Novel.

A pattern also qualifies if it validates a hypothesis the user wanted to confirm. Interesting patterns represent knowledge.

  • Objective measures use the structure and statistics of the pattern: rule support = P(X ∪ Y), confidence = P(Y | X) (each with a user threshold); for classification rules, accuracy and coverage; for understandability, simple measures such as pattern length.
  • Subjective measures reflect the user’s beliefs: a pattern is interesting if it is unexpected (contradicts a belief) or actionable (the user can act on it, such as “a large earthquake often follows a cluster of small quakes”). Objective measures alone can flag common sense as interesting.

Three questions: (1) What makes a pattern interesting? (above). (2) Can a system generate all interesting patterns? This is completeness; it is unrealistic, so constraints and interestingness measures focus the search (enough for completeness in association mining). (3) Can it generate only interesting patterns? An optimization problem that is still hard. Interestingness measures can rank and filter patterns after mining and, more importantly, prune the search during mining.

Han et al., §1.4.6 “Are All Patterns Interesting?”; Chapter 6, §6.3 (lift, χ² and Kulczynski). Notes: 22/06/26 (“pattern evaluation: identify which patterns are more interesting”).

1.5 Which technologies are used?

Statistics, machine learning, databases, information retrieval

Data mining draws on statistics, machine learning, pattern recognition, database and data warehouse systems, information retrieval, visualization, algorithms and high-performance computing (Figure 1.11).

  • Statistics: a statistical model describes the behaviour of a target class by random variables and probability distributions. Uses: build models of classes (characterization, classification), model noise and missing values, descriptive and inferential statistics, and verify results by hypothesis testing (a result is statistically significant if unlikely to have occurred by chance). Challenge: scaling statistical methods to large, distributed or real-time data.
  • Machine learning: supervised learning ≈ classification (labelled examples supervise); unsupervised ≈ clustering (unlabelled input; the clusters have no semantic names); semi-supervised uses labelled and unlabelled data (unlabelled points refine the decision boundary and reveal noise); active learning asks a user to label chosen examples. Difference from data mining: ML focuses on model accuracy; data mining adds efficiency, scalability and complex data.
  • Database systems and data warehouses: scalable storage, query processing and indexing for large structured data; warehouses consolidate sources and time frames into partially materialized cubes, supporting OLAP and multidimensional mining.
  • Information retrieval: searching documents; data are unstructured and queries are keywords (unlike SQL). Documents as a bag of words with a language model; a topic model is a distribution over the vocabulary, and documents are mixtures of topics. Leads to text and multimedia mining.

Han et al., §1.5 “Which Technologies Are Used?”, §1.5.1–§1.5.4, Figures 1.11–1.12.

1.6 Applications and 1.7 major issues

1.6 Business intelligence and Web search engines

Business intelligence (BI) gives historical, current and predictive views of operations (reporting, OLAP, performance management, competitive intelligence, benchmarking, predictive analytics). Data mining is its core: OLAP relies on warehousing and multidimensional mining; classification and prediction power predictive analytics; clustering groups customers for customer relationship management; characterization describes each group to design reward programs.

Web search engines are very large data mining applications, used in crawling (which pages, how often), indexing, and ranking, advertising and personalization. Three challenges: (1) huge, growing data needing clouds of computers; (2) online data and models that must be fast enough for real-time queries and incrementally updated on streams (for example a query classifier for “apple”); (3) rare queries (most queries are asked once or a few times), giving severely skewed data.

Han et al., §1.6 “Which Kinds of Applications Are Targeted?”, §1.6.1 “Business Intelligence”, §1.6.2 “Web Search Engines”.

Practice and exam toolkit

Model answers to the chapter’s exercises (1.1–1.9)

1.1 What is data mining? (a) Not just hype: it solves real problems (flu tracking, fraud detection, market basket analysis), though early marketing oversold it. (b) Not a simple re-application: scale, mixed data types, noise, and the need for efficient, scalable algorithms and interestingness measures made it a field of its own, though it draws on databases, statistics, machine learning and pattern recognition. (c) It is also an evolution of machine learning, statistics and pattern recognition: it applies their model-building methods to very large data. (d) The seven KDD steps (section 1.2).

1.2 Data warehouse vs database. Differences: a warehouse supports decisions (OLAP); it is subject-oriented, integrated from many sources, historical, summarized, multidimensional, mostly read-only; a database supports operations (OLTP); it holds current detailed data in ER tables with short read-write transactions. Similarities: both are data repositories built on DBMS technology (storage, indexing, query processing), and a warehouse is often implemented on a relational DBMS.

1.3, 1.4 Functionalities with examples (a store): characterization (profile of big spenders), discrimination (frequent vs rare computer buyers), association (computer ⇒ software), classification (predict a customer’s credit risk), regression (predict revenue of an item during a sale), clustering (customer segments), outlier analysis (fraudulent card use).

1.5 Differences and similarities. Discrimination vs classification: both separate classes; discrimination compares class descriptions in summarized terms for users, while classification builds a model to label new objects. Characterization vs clustering: both summarize groups; characterization describes a given class, clustering discovers groups in unlabelled data. Classification vs regression: both predictive and supervised; classification predicts categorical labels, regression predicts continuous values.

1.7 Outliers in fraud detection (two methods). (i) Statistical: model each account’s normal charges and flag charges far in the tail. (ii) Distance or density based: cluster accounts or transactions and flag objects remote from any cluster. Neither is always more reliable: statistical tests need a correct distribution assumption, distance methods need a sensible distance, and density methods find local outliers. Use them together and validate on known fraud cases.

1.8 Three challenges (methodology and user interaction). Handling noise, uncertainty and incompleteness; deciding which of the many patterns are interesting; and giving users interactive control, background knowledge and clear presentation of results.

1.9 Huge versus small data. Efficiency and scalability (sublinear or parallel algorithms, sampling, incremental mining), data that do not fit in memory or live on many sites, data quality at scale, and presenting results from billions of tuples.

Common mistakes and quick sheet
  • Writing the KDD steps out of order, or forgetting that steps 1–4 are preprocessing.
  • Mixing characterization (one class) with discrimination (target against contrasting classes).
  • Calling clustering “supervised”: it has no class labels.
  • Describing classification as predicting numbers: that is regression.
  • Saying data mining is just querying: queries retrieve what you ask for, mining discovers patterns you did not specify.
  • Forgetting “interesting” has four properties: understandable, valid, useful, novel.
support(X⇒Y) = P(X ∪ Y) confidence(X⇒Y) = P(Y | X) descriptive: characterization, discrimination, frequent patterns, clustering predictive : classification, regression interesting = understandable + valid + useful + novel objective measures: support, confidence, accuracy, coverage; subjective: unexpected, actionable
