2.1 Data objects and attribute types

2.1.1 Objects, attributes and the vocabulary

A data object is an entity (customer, patient, student). Synonyms: sample, example, instance, data point, tuple (rows of a database). An attribute is a data field describing a characteristic of an object. Attribute, dimension (data warehousing), feature (machine learning) and variable (statistics) are used interchangeably. Observed values are observations. The set of attributes describing an object is the attribute vector (feature vector). One attribute gives a univariate distribution, two give bivariate.

The type of an attribute is set by its possible values: nominal, binary, ordinal or numeric.

Han et al., Ch. 2 “Getting to Know Your Data”, §2.1 “Data Objects and Attribute Types”, §2.1.1 “What Is an Attribute?”. Notes: 25/06/26.

  • Interval vs ratio test: can you say “twice as much”? 10 °C is not twice as warm as 5 °C (no true zero), but $100 is 100 times $1.
  • Ordinal attributes can come from discretizing a numeric range into ordered categories.

2.1.6 Discrete versus continuous

A discrete attribute has a finite or countably infinite set of values (hair colour, smoker, age 0 to 110, customer ID, zip codes). A continuous attribute is one that is not discrete: real numbers, represented with finitely many digits as floating point. The classifications overlap, so a discrete attribute can be numeric (0 and 1, ages) and “numeric” and “continuous” are often used loosely as the same thing.

Han et al., §2.1.6 “Discrete versus Continuous Attributes”.

2.2 Basic statistical descriptions

2.2.1 Mean, weighted mean, trimmed mean, median, mode, midrange

Running data (Example 2.6, salary in $ thousands): 30, 36, 47, 50, 52, 52, 56, 60, 63, 70, 70, 110.

Mean x̄ = Σxᵢ / N = 696 / 12 = 58 Weighted mean = Σ wᵢxᵢ / Σ wᵢ (weights = importance or frequency) Trimmed mean: sort, chop the top and bottom ~2%, then average (do not trim too much, such as 20% at both ends) Median: sorted data; odd N → middle value; even N → average of the two middle values here (52 + 56)/2 = 54; first 11 values only → 6th value = 52 Mode: most frequent value(s): 52 and 70 → bimodal (unimodal, bimodal, trimodal, multimodal) Midrange = (max + min)/2 = (110 + 30)/2 = 70
  • The mean is the single most useful measure but is sensitive to outliers; use the median for skewed data.
  • Empirical relation for unimodal, moderately skewed data: mean − mode ≈ 3 × (mean − median).
  • Symmetric: mean = median = mode. Positively skewed (long right tail): mode < median < mean. Negatively skewed (long left tail): mean < median < mode.

Approximate median from grouped data (Eq. 2.3): median ≈ L₁ + ((N/2 − Σfreqlower) / freqmedian) × width, where L₁ is the lower boundary of the median interval (the interval containing the N/2-th value), Σfreqlower is the total frequency of all lower intervals, and width is the width of the median interval. Worked in the Practice section (Exercise 2.3).

Han et al., §2.2.1 “Measuring the Central Tendency: Mean, Median, and Mode”, Eqs. (2.1)–(2.4), Examples 2.6–2.9, Figure 2.1. Notes: 02/07/26.

2.2.2 Quartiles: four methods and why the answer can change

Quantiles are points at regular intervals that split sorted data into equal-size consecutive sets. There are q − 1 q-quantiles. The 2-quantile is the median; the 4-quantiles are the quartiles Q1, Q2, Q3 (25th, 50th, 75th percentiles); the 100-quantiles are percentiles. IQR = Q3 − Q1, the spread of the middle half. Range = max − min.

The book defines the kth q-quantile as a value with at most k/q of the data below it, but it does not fix one rule for even-sized data, so tools differ. Four common ways, on the two 12-value past-paper sets:

Outlier rule: a value is a suspected outlier if it is at least 1.5 × IQR above Q3 or below Q1. This gives x ≤ Q1 − 1.5·IQR (low) and x ≥ Q3 + 1.5·IQR (high).

Five-number summary: Minimum, Q1, Median, Q3, Maximum.

Boxplot rules (book): the box runs from Q1 to Q3 (length = IQR), the median is a line in the box, and the whiskers extend to the smallest and largest observations only if they are within 1.5 × IQR of the quartiles; otherwise the whiskers stop at the most extreme observations inside that limit and the remaining points are plotted individually. Example 2.11: for branch 1 the median is $80, Q1 $60, Q3 $100, IQR 40, and 175 and 202 are plotted as outliers. This is why your S24 1a answer lists whisker ends −50 and 115 with −110, 268, 607 plotted separately.

Variance σ² = (1/N) Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² = (1/N) Σxᵢ² − x̄² σ = √σ² Example 2.12: σ² = (30² + 36² + … + 110²)/12 − 58² ≈ 379.17, σ ≈ 19.47
  • σ measures spread about the mean, so use it only when the mean is the chosen centre. σ = 0 only if all values are equal.
  • Use N for a whole population, and N − 1 for a sample (unbiased). With N − 1 the same data give variance 413.64 and σ = 20.34.
  • Chebyshev: at least (1 − 1/k²) × 100% of observations lie within k standard deviations of the mean.

2.2.3 Graphic displays: quantile plot, q-q plot, histogram, scatter plot
  • Quantile plot: sort the data and plot each xᵢ against fᵢ = (i − 0.5)/N, the approximate fraction of the data below xᵢ. It shows all values and marks Q1, the median and Q3 at f = 0.25, 0.50, 0.75.
  • Quantile–quantile (q-q) plot: plots the quantiles of one univariate distribution against the corresponding quantiles of another. If the sample sizes differ, interpolate to the smaller one. Points on the straight line mean equal quantiles; points below the line mean the first distribution is shifted lower (Example 2.14: branch 1 prices are lower than branch 2).
  • Histogram: equal-width buckets, bar height = count. For nominal data it is a bar chart. Less effective than quantile plots, q-q plots and boxplots for comparing groups.
  • Scatter plot: pairs of values as points; shows positive (lower-left to upper-right), negative (upper-left to lower-right) or no correlation, plus clusters and outliers. Quantile plots, q-q plots and histograms show one attribute; scatter plots show two.

Han et al., §2.2.3 “Graphic Displays of Basic Statistical Descriptions of Data”, Eq. (2.7), Examples 2.13–2.15, Figures 2.4–2.9.

2.4 Measuring similarity and dissimilarity

2.4.1 Data matrix vs dissimilarity matrix; similarity vs dissimilarity

Proximity means similarity or dissimilarity. Similarity: higher means more alike, often in [0, 1], 1 for identical. Dissimilarity (such as distance): 0 for identical, higher means more different, upper limit varies. Typically sim(i, j) = 1 − d(i, j).

  • Data matrix (object-by-attribute): n objects × p attributes, an n × p matrix, rows are feature vectors. Two-mode (objects and attributes).
  • Dissimilarity matrix (object-by-object): n × n of d(i, j), with d(i, i) = 0 and d(i, j) = d(j, i), so only the lower triangle is shown. One-mode. Many clustering and nearest-neighbour algorithms use it, and a data matrix can be converted into one.

Use of proximity: clustering, outlier detection (objects dissimilar to all others) and nearest-neighbour classification.

Han et al., §2.4 “Measuring Data Similarity and Dissimilarity”, §2.4.1 “Data Matrix versus Dissimilarity Matrix”, Eqs. (2.8)–(2.10); Tan et al., chapter “Data”, section “Measures of Similarity and Dissimilarity”. Notes: 09/07/26.

2.4.2 Nominal attributes (simple matching)
d(i, j) = (p − m) / p sim(i, j) = m / p p = number of nominal attributes, m = number of attributes where i and j match

Symmetric binary: d(i,j) = (r + s) / (q + r + s + t) Asymmetric binary: d(i,j) = (r + s) / (q + r + s) (0-0 matches, t, are ignored) Jaccard coefficient: sim(i,j) = q / (q + r + s) = 1 − d(i,j) (asymmetric)

q = both 1, r = i has 1 and j has 0, s = i has 0 and j has 1, t = both 0, p = q + r + s + t. Treating binary attributes as numeric can mislead, and a positive match (1-1) is more informative than a negative match (0-0) for rare outcomes.

Jack and Mary are the most similar; Jim and Mary the least.

2.4.4 Numeric attributes: Euclidean, Manhattan, Minkowski, supremum
Euclidean (h=2): d(i,j) = √( Σ (xᵢf − xⱼf)² ) Manhattan (h=1): d(i,j) = Σ |xᵢf − xⱼf| Minkowski: d(i,j) = ( Σ |xᵢf − xⱼf|^h )^(1/h), h ≥ 1 Supremum (h→∞): d(i,j) = max over f of |xᵢf − xⱼf| (Chebyshev, L∞, uniform norm) Weighted Euclidean: √( w₁|·|² + w₂|·|² + … )
  • A metric satisfies non-negativity, d(i,i) = 0, symmetry, and the triangle inequality d(i,j) ≤ d(i,k) + d(k,j). Euclidean, Manhattan, Minkowski and supremum are metrics.
  • Normalize first (Chapter 3) so a large-range attribute (income, or height in inches) does not dominate. Your slides also show standardizing with the z-score or with the mean absolute deviation, which is more robust to outliers.

Examples 2.19–2.20: x₁ = (1, 2), x₂ = (3, 5): Euclidean √(2² + 3²) = 3.61; Manhattan 2 + 3 = 5; supremum = max(2, 3) = 3 (the second attribute gives the biggest gap).

Han et al., §2.4.4 “Dissimilarity of Numeric Data: Minkowski Distance”, Eqs. (2.16)–(2.20), Examples 2.19–2.20, Figure 2.23. Notes: 15/07/26.

2.4.5 Ordinal attributes (past-paper favourite)
  1. Replace each value by its rank r (1 … M).
  2. Normalize to [0, 1]: z = (r − 1)/(M − 1), so every ordinal attribute has equal weight.
  3. Use a numeric distance (Euclidean, Manhattan) on z.

Example 2.21: test-2 values excellent, fair, good, excellent with M = 3 (fair = 1, good = 2, excellent = 3) give ranks 3, 1, 2, 3 and z = 1, 0, 0.5, 1.

1 2 3 4 1 0 2 1.0 0 3 0.5 0.5 0 4 0 1.0 0.5 0 (objects 1 and 2, and 2 and 4, are the most dissimilar)

Same method for the past-paper “Service Level” and “Grade” matrices: Low, Medium, High map to 0, 0.5, 1 and fair, good, excellent map to 0, 0.5, 1. With several ordinal attributes, compute z for each and then use a distance such as Manhattan or Minkowski (your 15/07 notes).

Han et al., §2.4.5 “Proximity Measures for Ordinal Attributes”, Eq. (2.21), Example 2.21. Notes: 15/07/26.

2.4.7 Cosine similarity for sparse vectors (Example 2.23)

Term-frequency vectors are long and sparse. Two documents sharing many zeros are not similar because of it, so we need a measure that ignores 0-0 matches: sim(x, y) = x·y / (‖x‖‖y‖), the cosine of the angle between the vectors. 0 means orthogonal (no match); closer to 1 means a better match. It is a nonmetric measure.

x = (5,0,3,0,2,0,0,2,0,0) y = (3,0,2,0,1,1,0,1,0,1) x·y = 15 + 6 + 2 + 2 = 25 ‖x‖ = 6.48 ‖y‖ = 4.12 sim(x, y) = 25 / (6.48 × 4.12) = 0.94

For binary vectors, x·y counts shared attributes. The variation Tanimoto coefficient sim = x·y / (x·x + y·y − x·y) is the number of shared attributes over the number possessed by x or y (the Jaccard form), common in information retrieval and biology.

Han et al., §2.4.7 “Cosine Similarity”, Eqs. (2.23)–(2.24), Example 2.23, Table 2.5.

Common mistakes and formula sheet
  • Computing quartiles by a different method from the one expected (see 2.2.2); always name your method.
  • Including the median in both halves, or forgetting to exclude it when N is odd (method A).
  • Using the mean for ordinal data, or any ratio on interval-scaled data.
  • Including 0-0 matches (t) in the asymmetric binary distance.
  • Forgetting to normalize ordinal ranks, z = (r − 1)/(M − 1).
  • Mixing up supremum (the maximum single-attribute difference) with Manhattan (the sum).
  • Using N where the question gives a sample (or the reverse) for variance; say which one you use.
