4.1 Data warehouse: basic concepts

4.1.1 What a data warehouse is (Inmon’s four features)

A data warehouse is a repository maintained separately from the operational databases, supporting decisions with consolidated historic data. Inmon’s definition: “a subject-oriented, integrated, time-variant, and nonvolatile collection of data in support of management’s decision making process.”

  • Subject-oriented: organized around subjects (customer, supplier, product, sales), not day-to-day operations; data not useful for decisions is excluded.
  • Integrated: built from heterogeneous sources (relational databases, flat files, online transaction records); cleaning and integration make names, encodings and measures consistent.
  • Time-variant: holds history (for example 5–10 years); every key structure has a time element.
  • Nonvolatile: physically separate from operational data, so it needs no transaction processing, recovery or concurrency control. Only two operations: initial loading and access.
Notes check: your 24/08 note says non-volatile means “has recovery and concurrency”. The book says the opposite: a warehouse does not need those mechanisms.

Update-driven vs query-driven integration (Exercise 4.1). The traditional query-driven approach puts wrappers and mediators on top of each source and translates each query at run time. It is complex, competes with the sources for resources, and is expensive for frequent aggregate queries. The warehouse uses an update-driven approach: data are integrated in advance and stored for direct querying, so queries are fast and do not disturb the sources. The trade-off is that the warehouse is not completely current.

Uses (the book’s list): increasing customer focus, repositioning products by comparing sales by quarter, year and region, analyzing operations and sources of profit, and managing customer relationships.

Han et al., Ch. 4 “Data Warehousing and Online Analytical Processing”, §4.1.1 “What Is a Data Warehouse?”; Inmon (1996), as cited in the book; Kimball & Ross, The Data Warehouse Toolkit, 3rd ed., Ch. 1 “Data Warehousing, Business Intelligence, and Dimensional Modeling Primer”. Why separate (three reasons from §4.1.3):

  1. Performance of both systems. Operational databases are tuned for known tasks (primary-key indexing, canned queries). Warehouse queries are complex aggregations over large groups; running them on the operational system would degrade it.
  2. Concurrency control and recovery. OLTP needs locking and logging. Applying these to read-only OLAP scans can jeopardize concurrent transactions and cut throughput.
  3. Different structure, content and use. Decision support needs historic, consolidated, clean data from many sources; operational databases hold only current detailed raw data.

Your notes add “merging them slows both”, which is reason 1. Faster market analysis needs less detail, which is reason 3.

4.1.5 Warehouse models and the recommended approach
  • Enterprise warehouse: all subjects across the organization; detailed plus summarized data; GBs to TBs; needs extensive business modeling and may take years.
  • Data mart: a subset for a specific group (for example marketing: customer, item, sales); usually summarized; low-cost departmental servers; weeks to build. Independent marts come from operational or local sources; dependent marts come from the enterprise warehouse.
  • Virtual warehouse: a set of views over operational databases, with only some summaries materialized. Easy to build but needs excess capacity on operational servers.
ApproachProsCons
Top-down (enterprise first)Systematic; minimizes integration problemsExpensive, slow, inflexible (hard to agree on one model)
Bottom-up (marts first)Flexible, low cost, quick return on investmentIntegrating disparate marts later can be hard

Recommended approach (incremental and evolutionary, Figure 4.2):

  1. Define a high-level corporate data model in a short period (one or two months): a corporate-wide, consistent view across subjects.
  2. Build independent data marts in parallel with the enterprise warehouse, based on the same model.
  3. Build distributed data marts that integrate the different marts via hub servers.
  4. Construct a multitier data warehouse: the enterprise warehouse is the sole custodian of all warehouse data, which is distributed to the dependent data marts.

For a three-tier design, add the bottom, middle and top tiers with their tools.

4.1.6–4.1.7 ETL back-end tools and the metadata repository
  • Data extraction: gathers data from multiple, heterogeneous, external sources.
  • Data cleaning: detects errors and rectifies them when possible.
  • Data transformation: converts from legacy or host format to warehouse format.
  • Load: sorts, summarizes, consolidates, computes views, checks integrity, builds indices and partitions.
  • Refresh: propagates updates from the sources to the warehouse.

Metadata is data about data. The repository holds: the warehouse structure (schema, views, dimensions, hierarchies, derived data, mart locations); operational metadata (lineage, currency of data as active, archived or purged, monitoring statistics); summarization algorithms; the mapping from sources (extraction, cleaning and transformation rules, refresh and purging rules, security); performance data (indices, profiles, refresh schedules); and business metadata (terms, ownership, charging policies). It is a directory for analysts and a guide for the data mapping. Store it persistently on disk.

Levels of summarization in a warehouse: current detailed data (on disk), older detailed data (tertiary storage), lightly summarized, highly summarized.

Han et al., §4.1.6 “Extraction, Transformation, and Loading”, §4.1.7 “Metadata Repository”. Notes: 27/08/26.

4.2 Data warehouse modeling: data cube and OLAP

4.2.1 The data cube, cuboids, base and apex, lattice

A data cube is defined by dimensions (perspectives such as time, item, branch, location; each may have a dimension table) and facts (numeric measures such as dollars sold, units sold, amount budgeted) kept in a fact table that holds the measure names plus a key to each dimension table. The cube is n-dimensional; “3-D” is only a metaphor.

How to choose in an exam: one subject and one set of measures means one fact table, so star or snowflake. “Minimize redundancy” points to snowflake; “query performance and clarity” points to star (the book notes the space saving of snowflake is negligible next to the fact table). 4.2.3 Concept hierarchies

A concept hierarchy maps low-level concepts to higher-level, more general ones (city → province or state → country).

  • Schema hierarchy, total order: street < city < province_or_state < country.
  • Schema hierarchy, partial order (lattice): day < {month < quarter; week} < year. A week often crosses month boundaries, so week is treated as a lower level of year, not of month (book footnote).
  • Set-grouping hierarchy: values grouped into ranges, such as price ($0…$1000] split into ($0…$200], …

There can be several hierarchies per dimension (different viewpoints), defined manually or generated automatically. Hierarchies let data be handled at varying levels of abstraction, which is what roll-up and drill-down use.

Han et al., §4.2.3 “Dimensions: The Role of Concept Hierarchies”, Figures 4.9–4.11.

4.2.4 Measures: distributive, algebraic, holistic

A measure is a numeric function evaluated at each point (a set of dimension–value pairs, such as ⟨Q1, Vancouver, computer⟩) by aggregating the matching data.

  • Distributive: split the data into n partitions, apply the function to each, then apply it to the n results; if this equals applying it to all the data, it is distributive. Examples: sum(), count(), min(), max().
  • Algebraic: computed from a bounded number M of distributive results. Examples: avg() = sum()/count(); min_N(), max_N(); standard deviation().
  • Holistic: no constant bound on the storage needed for a sub-aggregate. Examples: median(), mode(), rank().

Most large cube applications need distributive and algebraic measures, which are efficient. Holistic measures are hard; approximations exist (for example the estimated median from Chapter 2, Eq. 2.3).

Incremental computation (Exercise 4.12): when new data are added, count and sum update by adding; standard deviation updates from the stored count, sum and sum of squares; the median cannot be updated from a few stored values, so all data must be revisited. Averaging averages is wrong unless the counts are carried along: avg is algebraic because it keeps sum and count.

Han et al., §4.2.4 “Measures: Their Categorization and Computation”; Exercise 4.12. Notes: 14/09/26.

4.2.5 OLAP operations, worked on the book’s data

OLAP operations materialize different views of a cube for interactive analysis.

  • Roll-up (drill-up): aggregate by climbing a hierarchy or by dimension reduction (removing a dimension).
  • Drill-down: the reverse: step down a hierarchy or add a dimension.
  • Slice: select on one dimension (a sub-cube). Dice: select on two or more dimensions.
  • Pivot (rotate): rotate the axes of a view.
  • Others: drill-across (queries over more than one fact table), drill-through (down to the back-end relational tables), top-N ranking, moving averages, growth rates.

4.3 Data warehouse design and usage

4.3.1–4.3.2 Business analysis framework and the design process

Four views must be considered: top-down (what the business needs), data source (what operational systems capture), data warehouse (fact and dimension tables) and business query (the end-user’s view).

  • Top-down: starts with overall planning; suits mature technology and well-understood problems.
  • Bottom-up: starts with experiments and prototypes; cheaper, lets you evaluate technology before big commitments.
  • Combined: planned and strategic, but with rapid implementation.

Waterfall does structured analysis at each step before the next; spiral makes quick, increasingly functional releases and suits data marts.

Four design steps:

  1. Choose a business process (orders, shipments, inventory, sales). Organization-wide means a warehouse; departmental means a data mart.
  2. Choose the grain: the atomic level of data in the fact table (individual transactions, daily snapshots).
  3. Choose the dimensions for each fact record (time, item, customer, supplier).
  4. Choose the measures (numeric additive quantities such as dollars sold and units sold).

Define the scope (budget, subsets, sources, departments), then plan deployment and administration (refresh, synchronization, disaster recovery, access control, performance).

Han et al., §4.3.1 “A Business Analysis Framework for Data Warehouse Design”, §4.3.2 “Data Warehouse Design Process”; Kimball & Ross, Ch. 3, “Four-Step Dimensional Design Process”. Notes: 14/09/26.

4.3.3–4.3.4 Usage, OLAP vs data mining, and OLAM

Three kinds of applications:

  • Information processing: querying, basic statistics, reports (crosstabs, tables, charts).
  • Analytical processing: OLAP operations on summarized and detailed historic data (slice and dice, drill, roll-up, pivot).
  • Data mining: hidden patterns and associations, classification, prediction, clustering, with visualization of results.

Information processing is not data mining. OLAP is user-directed summarization and comparison; data mining automates discovery of implicit patterns and goes beyond OLAP (association, classification, clustering, time series, and data that does not fit a cube, such as text).

Multidimensional data mining (OLAM, online analytical mining) integrates OLAP with mining, for four reasons: (1) warehouse data are clean and integrated, saving preprocessing; (2) existing infrastructure surrounds the warehouse; (3) OLAP-based exploration lets users mine different subsets at different abstraction levels; (4) users can select and swap mining functions online. Example: roll up items to brands, or transactions to customer types, before mining associations.

Han et al., §4.3.3 “Data Warehouse Usage for Information Processing”, §4.3.4 “From Online Analytical Processing to Multidimensional Data Mining”; Exercise 4.17.

4.4 Data warehouse implementation

4.4.1 Cube computation, cuboid counts, and materialization

Each group-by is a cuboid. The compute cube operator computes aggregates for all subsets of the listed dimensions (the n-dimensional generalization of group-by).

dimensions (city, item, year), measure sum(sales) → 2³ = 8 cuboids {(city,item,year), (city,item), (city,year), (item,year), (city), (item), (year), ()} Total cuboids with hierarchies = Π (Lᵢ + 1) (+1 for the virtual top level "all") 10 dimensions × 5 levels (incl. all): 5¹⁰ ≈ 9.8 × 10⁶ cuboids

Storage can explode: the curse of dimensionality. A cuboid’s size also depends on cardinalities (a city × item group-by can have |city| × |item| rows).

  • No materialization: compute non-base cuboids on the fly; slow.
  • Full materialization: precompute all (the full cube); needs huge storage.
  • Partial materialization: compute a proper subset of cuboids, or a subcube (only some cells). Choose by workload, frequencies, access costs, update cost and storage. Heuristics: materialize the cuboid on which many frequent cuboids are based; an iceberg cube stores only cells whose aggregate (such as count) passes a threshold; a shell cube precomputes cuboids on only a few dimensions (3–5).

Check yourself (derived here): a cube with n dimensions and exactly p distinct values per dimension (no hierarchies) has at most pⁿ base cells, and at most (p + 1)ⁿ cells in total, because each dimension can take one of p values or “all”.

Han et al., §4.4.1 “Efficient Data Cube Computation: An Overview”, Example 4.6, Eq. (4.1); Gray et al. (1997), the CUBE operator, and Harinarayan et al. (1996), cuboid selection, as cited in the book. Notes: 14/09/26, 15/09/26.

4.4.2 Bitmap and join indexes

Bitmap index: for an attribute with n distinct values, keep n bit vectors; a row’s bit in vector v is 1 if the row has value v. Comparison, join and aggregation become bit arithmetic, saving time, space and I/O. It is best for low-cardinality attributes (compression adapts it to high cardinality) and beats hash and tree indices there.

Base table: R1 H V | R2 C V | R3 P V | R4 S V | R5 H T | R6 C T | R7 P T | R8 S T item index H C P S : R1 1000 R2 0100 R3 0010 R4 0001 R5 1000 R6 0100 R7 0010 R8 0001 city index V T : R1–R4 = 10, R5–R8 = 01 Query item = C AND city = T : (C column 01000100) AND (T column 00001111) = 00000100 → R6

Sizing example: a cube stored as 100,000 rows with four item values needs four bit vectors of 100,000 bits.

Join index: records pairs of joinable row IDs (RID, SID) from two relations, avoiding costly joins. In a star schema it links a dimension value to the fact rows that match it. Example 4.8: “Main Street” joins fact tuples T57, T238, T884; “Sony-TV” joins T57 and T459; a composite join index on (Main Street, Sony-TV) gives T57. Bitmapped join indices combine both methods.

Han et al., §4.4.2 “Indexing OLAP Data: Bitmap Index and Join Index”, Examples 4.7–4.8, Figures 4.15–4.17. Notes: 15/09/26.

4.4.3 Processing OLAP queries (choosing a cuboid)

Steps: (1) translate selection, projection, roll-up and drill-down into operations on available cuboids; (2) pick the materialized cuboid(s) that can answer the query, prune using dominance, estimate costs, and take the cheapest.

Example 4.9: query on {brand, province_or_state} with year = 2010. Materialized cuboids: (1) {year, item_name, city}; (2) {year, brand, country}; (3) {year, brand, province_or_state}; (4) {item_name, province_or_state} where year = 2010.

  • Cuboid 2 is unusable: country is more general than province_or_state, and finer data cannot be built from coarser data.
  • Cuboids 1, 3 and 4 qualify: same or more dimensions, the selection can be implied, and item and location are at a finer or equal level.
  • Cuboid 1 is likely the costliest (both dimensions far finer than needed). Cuboid 3 is usually smaller than 4, so it is chosen, unless efficient indices on cuboid 4 make it cheaper. A cost estimate decides.

Han et al., §4.4.3 “Efficient Processing of OLAP Queries”, Example 4.9.

4.5 Attribute-oriented induction (summary only)

Data generalization abstracts a large set of task-relevant data from low to higher conceptual levels, by data cube aggregation or by attribute-oriented induction (AOI). Concept description has two forms: characterization (summarize a target class) and comparison/discrimination (distinguish the target class from contrasting classes). AOI techniques: data focusing, attribute removal or attribute generalization, count and aggregate accumulation, generalization control, and visualization. Common mistakes and formula sheet
  • Forgetting the +1 for “all” in Π(Lᵢ + 1), or counting “all” as a real level in Lᵢ.
  • Mixing up slice (one dimension) and dice (two or more).
  • Calling roll-up and drill-up different things: they are the same operation.
  • Saying a warehouse needs concurrency control and recovery; it does not.
  • Choosing snowflake “for speed”: it saves space but needs more joins.
  • Treating the median as algebraic: it is holistic.
  • Drawing a schema without foreign keys and measures in the fact table.
