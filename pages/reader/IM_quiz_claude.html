CHAPTER 13

Breakeven & Payback Analysis

Engineering Economy — finding the value of a parameter that makes two things equal, and how fast an investment pays for itself.

13–1LEARNING OUTCOMES
01
Breakeven point — one parameter
Finding the single value of a variable (like quantity) that makes a project break even.
02
Breakeven point — two alternatives
Finding the value of a common variable at which two competing alternatives cost the same.
03
Payback period analysis
Estimating how long it takes cash inflows to recover an initial investment.
13–2 / 13–4WHAT IS A BREAKEVEN POINT

Value of a parameter that makes two elements equal

The parameter can be revenue, cost, supply, demand, or almost anything else — for a single project, or for a comparison between two alternatives.

ONE PROJECT

The breakeven point is identified as QBE, determined using linear or non-linear relations for revenue and cost.

TWO ALTERNATIVES

Determine one of the parameters — P, A, F, i, or n — while holding the others constant.

A breakeven value can be found by any of three routes:

Direct solution of relations Trial and error Spreadsheet functions or tools
13–4COST–REVENUE MODEL — ONE PROJECT

Fixed cost, variable cost, revenue, profit

FIXED COST, FC

Costs not directly dependent on the variable — buildings, fixed overhead, insurance, minimum workforce cost.

VARIABLE COST, VC

Costs that change with the parameter — labor, material, marketing. Variable cost per unit is v.

TOTAL COST, TC

Sum of fixed and variable costs: TC = FC + VC

REVENUE, R

Amount dependent on quantity sold. Revenue per unit is r.

Profit, P = R – TC = R – (FC + VC)
13–5BREAKEVEN FOR LINEAR R AND TC

Set R = TC and solve for Q = QBE

R = TC rQ = FC + vQ FC Q_BE = ───── r – v

When the variable cost, v, is lowered, QBE decreases — the breakeven point moves left, so the project turns a profit sooner.

13–6WORKED EXAMPLE — ONE-PROJECT BREAKEVEN

A plant producing above its breakeven level

A plant produces 15,000 units/month. FC = $75,000/month, revenue is $8/unit, and variable cost is $2.50/unit. Find the breakeven level and the expected monthly profit or loss.

SOLUTION
Q_BE = FC / (r – v) = 75,000 / (8.00 – 2.50)
Q_BE = 13,636 units/month
Production (15,000) is above breakeven (13,636), so the plant is profitable.
Profit = (r – v)Q – FC = (8.00 – 2.50)(15,000) – 75,000
Profit = $7,500 / month
13–7BREAKEVEN BETWEEN TWO ALTERNATIVES

Finding the value of a common variable

  1. 1. Define the common variable.
  2. 2. Develop equivalence PW, AW, or FW relations as a function of that variable, for each alternative.
  3. 3. Equate the relations and solve for the variable — this is the breakeven value.

VALUE BELOW BREAKEVEN

  • Select the alternative with the higher variable cost — the larger slope.

VALUE ABOVE BREAKEVEN

  • Select the alternative with the lower variable cost.
13–15WORKED EXAMPLE — MAKE VS. BUY

Two-alternative breakeven analysis

Common variable X = units produced per year. AW relations:

AW_make = -18,000(A/P,15%,6) + 2,000(A/F,15%,6) - 0.4X AW_buy = -1.5X
SOLUTION — EQUATE AND SOLVE FOR X
-1.5X = -4,528 - 0.4X
X = 4,116 units / year
DECISION RULE If anticipated production > 4,116 units/year, select Make (the lower variable-cost option). Below it, select Buy.
13–11PAYBACK PERIOD ANALYSIS

How fast an investment recovers itself

Payback period: the estimated amount of time, np, for cash inflows to recover an initial investment, P, plus a stated rate of return, i%.

NO-RETURN PAYBACK

Rate of return is treated as zero (i = 0%).

DISCOUNTED PAYBACK

Considers the time value of money (i > 0%).

CAUTION Payback period analysis is a good initial screening tool — not the primary method for justifying a project or choosing between alternatives.
13–13POINTS TO REMEMBER

Limits of payback analysis

—
No-return payback neglects the time value of money, so no return is expected on the investment made.
—
Cash flows after the payback period aren't considered — the true return may be higher if those later flows are positive.
—
The payback approach differs fundamentally from PW, AW, ROR, and B/C analysis — it can lead to selecting a different alternative than they would.
—
Rely on payback as a supplemental tool; use PW or AW at the MARR for the reliable decision.
—
Discounted payback (i > 0%) gives a better sense of the risk involved.
CHAPTER 14

Supply Chain, Structure & People

How goods and information move through a chain of firms, and how the people and structures inside any one firm are organized, coordinated, and led.

SCM–01FOUNDATIONS

What is a supply chain?

Every party involved, directly or indirectly, in fulfilling a customer request — not just manufacturers and suppliers, but transporters, warehouses, retailers, and customers themselves.

DYNAMIC, NOT STATIC

Constant flow of products, information, and funds between stages.

CUSTOMER-TRIGGERED

Every chain exists to satisfy a customer need — and generate profit while doing it.

OBJECTIVE

Maximize total supply-chain surplus, not the profit of any single stage.

SCM–02 / 03CYCLIC & PUSH/PULL VIEWS

Two ways to view supply-chain processes

Cyclic view: every process is a series of cycles performed at the interface between two successive stages.

PROCUREMENT

Manufacturer orders components → supplier production → shipping → receiving.

MANUFACTURING

Order arrival → production scheduling → manufacturing → shipping.

REPLENISHMENT

Retailer order trigger → distributor fulfillment → receiving.

CUSTOMER ORDER

Customer arrival → order entry → fulfillment → receiving.

Push/pull view: processes are classified by whether they run ahead of, or in response to, an actual order.

PUSH PROCESSES

Executed in anticipation of demand — forecast-driven, speculative, needs safety stock.

  • Raw material procurement
  • Production scheduling
  • Manufacturing
  • Primary distribution

PULL PROCESSES

Executed in response to an actual order — reactive, demand-driven.

  • Order fulfillment
  • Final assembly (some chains)
  • Last-mile delivery

The push–pull boundary's position depends on demand uncertainty and lead time. A make-to-stock chain pushes further (boundary near the customer); a make-to-order chain pulls further upstream. Moving the boundary upstream cuts inventory risk but raises response time.

SCM–04PERFORMANCE FRAMEWORK

Six major drivers of supply-chain performance

These drivers determine the responsiveness–efficiency trade-off of the entire chain.

FACILITIES

Locations where inventory is stored, assembled, or fabricated — plants and warehouses.

INVENTORY

Raw materials, WIP, and finished goods held to buffer mismatches.

TRANSPORTATION

Moving inventory between stages; mode choice trades cost against speed.

INFORMATION

Data on demand, forecasts, inventory, and capacity that coordinates all other drivers.

SOURCING

Deciding who performs each activity — make vs. buy, supplier selection.

PRICING

How pricing decisions shape the demand patterns customers exhibit.

SCM–05COORDINATION

Lack of coordination in the supply chain

Coordination exists when every stage acts to maximize total supply-chain profit. Poor coordination arises from misaligned objectives or distorted information.

CAUSES

  • Incentive misalignment across independently owned stages
  • Information processing errors (forecasts, orders, lead times)
  • Operational and financial inefficiency between stages
  • Order batching — large, infrequent orders to save on fixed costs
  • Price fluctuations — promotions and forward buying
  • Rationing and shortage gaming during supply constraints

EFFECTS

  • Manufacturing cost ↑ — erratic orders force excess capacity or overtime
  • Inventory cost ↑ — more safety stock to buffer variability
  • Replenishment lead time ↑ — overloaded stages fall behind
  • Transportation cost ↑ — irregular shipments break full-load efficiency
  • Product availability ↓ — stockouts despite higher total inventory
  • Relationships across the chain ↓ — trust erodes, information sharing drops
SCM–06 / 08COORDINATION FAILURE IN ACTION

The bullwhip effect

The single most-cited symptom of poor supply-chain coordination: order fluctuations amplify as they move upstream, away from the end customer.

Root cause: each stage forecasts and orders independently, amplifying small demand signals — an information problem, not a demand problem.

Cause Why it happens Countermeasure
Demand forecast updating Each stage forecasts from the order it receives, not true demand, compounding error upstream. Share point-of-sale data; forecast from actual demand.
Order batching Periodic or lot-size ordering creates lumpy, infrequent orders. Enable smaller, more frequent replenishment; use third-party consolidation.
Price fluctuation Trade promotions and forward buying drive large, irregular purchases. Stabilize pricing; move toward every-day-low-pricing.
Rationing & shortage gaming Inflated orders during shortages misrepresent real demand. Allocate based on past sales; share capacity/inventory data.
SCM–07CASE STUDIES

Bullwhip effect in the real world

PROCTER & GAMBLE — PAMPERS

Problem: retail diaper demand was famously steady, yet factory orders from distributors swung far more sharply than actual sell-through.

Response: a Continuous Replenishment Program sharing point-of-sale data directly with retailers.

Result: order variability compressed sharply — lower inventory and fewer stockouts together.

BARILLA SPA — VENDOR-MANAGED INVENTORY

Problem: trade promotions and volume discounts led to large, irregular distributor orders despite stable end-consumer demand.

Response: a VMI pilot using retailers' actual sell-through data to set shipment quantities.

Result: smoother production and lower inventory chain-wide.

ORG–01ORGANIZATION: DESIGN & STRUCTURE

Six building blocks of organizational design

WORK SPECIALIZATION

Dividing tasks into narrow, repeatable jobs rather than one person doing the whole job.

DEPARTMENTALIZATION

Grouping jobs by function, product, geography, process, or customer.

CHAIN OF COMMAND

The unbroken line of authority from top to bottom, clarifying who reports to whom.

SPAN OF CONTROL

How many subordinates a manager can efficiently and effectively direct.

CENTRALIZATION

How much decision-making authority sits at upper levels vs. is pushed down.

FORMALIZATION

The degree to which jobs are standardized via rules, procedures, and documentation.

ORG–02COMMON ORGANIZATIONAL STRUCTURES

The same building blocks, four recurring forms

FUNCTIONAL

Groups people by specialty. Efficient, but silos can slow cross-department work.

DIVISIONAL

Groups by product, region, or customer segment — each division runs semi-autonomously.

MATRIX

Dual reporting lines: employees answer to both a functional manager and a project manager.

FLAT

Few management layers, wide spans of control — faster decisions, more autonomy.

ORG–03SPAN OF CONTROL & CHAIN OF COMMAND

Levels and span trade off directly

TALL STRUCTURE

Many levels, narrow span (~3–5 direct reports). Tight control, slower decisions, higher coordination cost.

FLAT STRUCTURE

Few levels, wide span (12+ direct reports). Faster decisions, more autonomy, harder to closely supervise.

ORG–04CENTRALIZATION VS. DECENTRALIZATION

Where decision-making authority sits

CENTRALIZATION

  • Consistent, standardized decisions
  • Strong top-down control
  • Simpler for small or stable firms
  • Slower response to local conditions

DECENTRALIZATION

  • Faster, locally-informed decisions
  • Builds lower-level management skill
  • Fits dynamic, complex environments
  • Risk of inconsistent execution

Organization size, environmental volatility, strategy, and the cost of a wrong decision all tip the balance — most firms centralize some decisions (e.g. finance) while decentralizing others (e.g. local pricing).

ORG–05 / 06COORDINATION IN MANAGEMENT

The glue that holds an organization together

Coordination is the process of synchronizing the activities of individuals and departments toward common goals — Mary Parker Follett called it "the glue that holds an organization together." Every managerial function — planning, organizing, staffing, directing — ultimately exists to coordinate effort, and the need grows with complexity.

— HORIZONTAL  ·  VERTICAL —
INTERNAL · VERTICALBetween hierarchy levels inside the firm — e.g. a plant manager aligning shift supervisors with head-office targets.
INTERNAL · HORIZONTALBetween departments at the same level — e.g. production and sales agreeing on a delivery schedule.
EXTERNAL · VERTICALUp/down a supply chain — e.g. a retailer aligning replenishment cycles with a supplier.
EXTERNAL · HORIZONTALWith outside peers — joint ventures, industry consortia, or regulator liaison.
1. DIRECT CONTACT

Coordinate through person-to-person communication, not just paperwork.

2. EARLY STAGE

Involve stakeholders while plans are still being formed.

3. RECIPROCAL RELATIONS

Every factor affects, and is affected by, every other factor.

4. CONTINUOUS PROCESS

Coordination is ongoing, not a one-time event.

Common techniques: committees & task forces, liaison roles, integrator/project-manager roles, standard rules & procedures, shared goals & plans, and shared information systems (ERP/MIS).

MOT–01…05MOTIVATION THEORIES

What drives effort, and how

Content theories focus on what motivates people (Maslow, Herzberg, McClelland). Process theories focus on how motivation happens (Vroom, Adams, McGregor).

Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory
Hygiene factors prevent dissatisfaction but don't motivate on their own: salary & job security, company policy, working conditions, relationships with supervisor/peers, status.

Motivators actively drive satisfaction and performance: achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement & growth.

Practical takeaway: fixing pay or conditions removes complaints, but only meaningful work, recognition, and growth actually motivate.
McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y
Theory X assumes employees inherently dislike work — must be directed and controlled, avoid responsibility, are motivated mainly by pay and job security, and are managed through close supervision.

Theory Y assumes employees find work naturally engaging — self-directed once committed, seek out responsibility, motivated by achievement and growth, and are managed through participative, empowering styles.

Neither is universally correct — a manager's assumptions about people shape the style they choose, and that style shapes how employees actually behave.
Vroom's Expectancy Theory & McClelland's Need Theory
Motivation Force = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence
Expectancy: will my effort lead to good performance? Instrumentality: will good performance lead to a reward? Valence: do I actually value that reward?

McClelland's need theory: everyone carries a mix of three learned needs — nAch (drive to excel and get feedback), nAff (desire for close relationships), nPow (desire to influence and control outcomes) — one usually dominates.
LEAD–01…04LEADERSHIP THEORIES

From who leaders are, to what they do, to it depends

1930s–40s

Trait theories — great leaders are born with fixed traits: intelligence, confidence, drive.

1940s–60s

Behavioral theories — leadership is a set of learnable behaviors.

1960s–80s

Contingency / situational — effectiveness depends on matching style to situation.

1980s–now

Modern theories — transformational, transactional, servant leadership.

Blake & Mouton's Managerial Grid

Plots leadership style on two independent behaviors: concern for people, and concern for production/results.

Fiedler's Contingency Model & Hersey–Blanchard's Situational Leadership
Fiedler: a leader's fixed style (task- or relationship-oriented) is matched to how favorable the situation is, based on leader–member relations, task structure, and position power. Very favorable or very unfavorable situations call for a task-oriented style; moderately favorable situations call for a relationship-oriented style.

Hersey & Blanchard: Telling (high task, low relationship — low-readiness followers) → Selling (high task, high relationship) → Participating (low task, high relationship) → Delegating (low task, low relationship — high-readiness followers).
Transformational vs. Transactional Leadership
Transformational leaders inspire followers to exceed self-interest for a larger vision: idealized influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, individualized consideration.

Transactional leaders manage performance through a clear exchange of reward for effort: contingent reward, active management by exception, passive management by exception, and laissez-faire (often excluded as "non-leadership").

Most effective leaders blend both — transactional structure for reliable execution, transformational vision for growth and change.
