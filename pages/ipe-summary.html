Industrial Management & Entrepreneurship: Exam Overview

A summary of everything in the class slides and the bundled textbooks, in your topic order. Formulas and worked numbers come from the slides. Book references (book, chapter, section) are at the end of each topic.

1Introduction to Management

  • Manager: works with and through other people to reach organizational goals. Three levels: first-line, middle, top managers.
  • Efficiency = “doing things right” (most output for least input). Effectiveness = “doing the right things” (reaching goals).
  • Four functions: Planning, Organizing, Leading, Controlling.
  • Mintzberg’s roles: Interpersonal (figurehead, leader, liaison); Informational (monitor, disseminator, spokesperson); Decisional (entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator).
  • Katz’s skills: Technical, Human, Conceptual. Technical matters more at lower levels, conceptual at the top, and human skills at every level.
  • Organization: a deliberate arrangement of people with a distinct purpose and a deliberate structure. The modern organization is flexible, team-oriented and customer-oriented, with diverse workers and networked relationships.
  • Challenges for managers: ethics, workforce diversity, globalization, e-business (enhanced, enabled, total), customer service, innovation, knowledge management and the learning organization.
  • Why study management: it is universal (all sizes and types of organization) and everyone either manages or is managed. Entrepreneurship is the organized effort to pursue opportunities and create value.
ReferencesRobbins, Coulter & Langton, Management (8th Canadian ed.), Ch. 1 “Introduction to Management and Organizations”. Also Stevenson, Operations Management, Ch. 1 “Introduction to Operations Management”.

2Inventory Management

  • Types: raw material, work-in-process (WIP), MRO (maintenance/repair/operating), finished goods. Inventory can be up to 50% of invested capital.
  • ABC analysis: rank items by annual dollar volume (units × unit cost). Class A is about 20% of items and 72% of dollars, B is about 30% of items and 23% of dollars, C is about 50% of items and 5% of dollars. Focus control on the few critical A items.
  • Costs: holding (carrying), ordering, and setup costs. Demand: independent vs dependent.

Basic EOQ model

Assumptions: constant, known demand; constant lead time; instantaneous receipt; no discounts; only ordering and holding costs vary; no stockouts.

Q* = √(2DS / H)   D = annual demand, S = cost per order, H = holding cost/unit/yr
Orders per year N = D/Q*   Cycle time T = working days / N
Total cost TC = (D/Q)S + (Q/2)H   At the optimum, ordering cost = holding cost

Example (needles): D = 1,000, S = $10, H = $0.50 → Q* = 200; N = 5; T = 50 days (250-day year); TC = $50 + $50 = $100.

Reorder point and safety stock

ROP = d × L   d = D / working days per year, L = lead time

Example (DVDs): D = 8,000, 250 days, L = 3 → d = 32/day → ROP = 96 units. Safety stock covers variable demand or lead time. Service level is the probability that demand will not exceed supply during lead time.

Production order quantity (EPQ)

Used when units are produced and used at the same time (production rate p > usage rate u). Inventory builds up gradually, so Imax < Q.

Quantity discount model

TC = (D/Q)S + (Q/2)H + P·D
  1. Compute Q* for each price.
  2. If Q* is below the discount threshold, raise it to the threshold quantity.
  3. Compute TC for each.
  4. Pick the lowest TC.

Example (cars): D = 5,000, S = $49, holding = 20% of price. Q* = 700, 714, 718 → adjusted to 700, 1,000, 2,000. TC = $25,700, $24,725, $24,822.50 → order 1,000 at $4.80.

ReferencesStevenson, Operations Management, Ch. 13 “Inventory Management”: sections “How Much to Order: Economic Order Quantity Models”, “Reorder Point Ordering”, “How Much to Order: Fixed-Order-Interval Model”, “The Single-Period Model”. Class slides follow a Prentice Hall deck (Stevenson 8th ed. named on the slide; Fig. 12.2 ABC curve).

3Facility Layout

  • Layout planning: the best physical arrangement of all resources that use space in a facility.
  • Four types: Process (group similar resources; flexible, general-purpose, labour-intensive, slower, higher handling cost, more space); Product (specialised line for one product; high capital, low flexibility, faster, lower handling cost and space); Hybrid (mix of both, e.g. group technology cells, grocery stores); Fixed-position (product is too large to move, so resources come to it).
  • Designing a process layout: (1) gather information: space needed and available, closeness importance (from-to matrix, REL chart); (2) develop alternative block plans by trial and error, or with ALDEP (based on the REL chart) or CRAFT (based on the from-to matrix); (3) develop a detailed layout.
  • Special cases: warehouse layouts (position departments relative to the dock; cross-docking) and office layouts (trade-off between proximity and privacy).

Designing a product layout (line balancing)

  1. Identify tasks and immediate predecessors.
  2. Set the desired output rate.
  3. Calculate the cycle time.
  4. Compute the theoretical minimum number of stations.
  5. Assign tasks to stations (longest eligible task first, without exceeding the cycle time).
  6. Compute efficiency and balance delay.
Cycle time = available time / desired output
TM = Σ task times / cycle time (always round up)
Efficiency = Σt / (N × cycle time)   Balance delay = 100% − efficiency

Example (Vicki’s Pizzeria): 60 pizzas/hr → cycle = 60 s. Total task time = 165 s → TM = 2.75 → 3 stations. Efficiency = 165/(3×60) = 91.7%; balance delay = 8.3%.

Group technology (GT) cells group products with similar processing needs, bringing product-layout efficiency into a process-layout shop and giving a more orderly flow.

ReferencesClass slides: “Chapter 10 – Facility Layout” (Wiley, 2007 slide set). Stevenson, Operations Management, Ch. 6 “Process Selection and Facility Layout”: sections “Strategic Resource Organization: Facilities Layout”, “Designing Product Layouts: Line Balancing”, “Designing Process Layouts”.

4Scheduling

  • Objective: trade off conflicting goals: efficient use of staff and equipment vs low waiting time, inventory and processing time. Good scheduling is a competitive advantage and gives reliable deliveries.
  • Priority (dispatching) rules: FCFS (first come, first served), SPT (shortest processing time), EDD (earliest due date), LPT (longest processing time).
Flow time = cumulative completion time   Lateness = max(0, flow time − due date)
Avg completion = Σflow / n   Utilization = Σprocessing / Σflow
Avg jobs in system = Σflow / Σprocessing   Avg lateness = Σlate days / n

Example jobs: A(6, due 8), B(2, 6), C(8, 18), D(3, 15), E(9, 23). Total work = 28 days.

RuleAvg completion (days)UtilizationJobs in systemAvg lateness
FCFS15.436.4%2.752.2
SPT13.043.1%2.321.8
EDD13.641.2%2.431.2
LPT20.627.2%3.689.6

Takeaways: no rule wins on every criterion. SPT is best for flow time and jobs in the system, but long jobs wait. EDD minimises lateness. FCFS is seen as fair. LPT is poor.

Johnson’s rule (N jobs, 2 machines)

Minimises total makespan and idle time. Repeat: pick the smallest time among all remaining jobs. If it is on machine 1, schedule that job as early as possible. If it is on machine 2, schedule it as late as possible. Remove the job and repeat.

Example (drill press, lathe): A(5,2) B(3,6) C(8,4) D(10,7) E(7,12) → sequence B-E-D-C-A, total time 35.

3 machines: Johnson’s rule applies only if min(M1) ≥ max(M2) or min(M3) ≥ max(M2). Then build two pseudo-machines, G = M1+M2 and H = M3+M2, and apply the rule to G and H.

Exam tip: for the 4-rule comparison, draw the flow-time table for each sequence first. Most marks are in the cumulative column.
ReferencesStevenson, Operations Management, Ch. 16 “Scheduling” (“Scheduling Operations”, “Scheduling in Low-Volume Systems”: sequencing, Johnson’s rule). Class slides follow Heizer & Render’s short-term scheduling deck (Prentice Hall).

5Forecasting

  • Horizons: short (up to 1 year, usually under 3 months: scheduling, workforce), medium (3 months to 3 years: sales and production planning, budgets), long (3+ years: new products, location, R&D).
  • Seven steps: determine use → select items → set horizon → select model → gather data → make forecast → validate and implement.
  • Qualitative (vague situation, little data, new products): jury of executive opinion, Delphi method (iterative anonymous expert panel), sales-force composite, market survey.
  • Quantitative: naive, moving average, exponential smoothing, trend projection, linear regression (associative).
  • Time-series components: trend, seasonal (within a year), cyclical (multi-year), random.
Naive: F(t+1) = A(t)
Moving average = Σ last n demands / n   (more n = smoother but slower to react)
Weighted MA = Σ(weight × demand) / Σweights
Exp. smoothing: F(t) = F(t−1) + α[A(t−1) − F(t−1)]   0 ≤ α ≤ 1

Examples: 3-month MA for sheds (10, 12, 13) → 11⅔. Weights 3/2/1 on (13, 12, 10) → 12⅙. Smoothing: F = 142, A = 153, α = 0.2 → 144.2.

Choosing α: high α (about 0.5) when the average is likely to change; low α (about 0.1) when it is stable. Typical range 0.05 to 0.50.

Trend-adjusted smoothing

F(t) = α·A(t−1) + (1−α)(F(t−1) + T(t−1))   T(t) = β(F(t) − F(t−1)) + (1−β)T(t−1)
FIT(t) = F(t) + T(t)

Example α = 0.2, β = 0.4: month 2 gives F = 12.8, T = 1.92, FIT = 14.72. Month 10 gives FIT = 35.16.

Least squares trend and error measures

ŷ = a + bx   b = (Σxy − n·x̄·ȳ)/(Σx² − n·x̄²)   a = ȳ − b·x̄
Error = A − F   MAD = Σ|e|/n   MSE = Σe²/n   MAPE = Σ(100|e|/A)/n

Power demand example gives ŷ = 56.70 + 10.54x, so year 8 ≈ 141 MW. The tonnage example with α = 0.10 gives MAD 10.31, MSE ≈ 190.8 and MAPE 5.59%. With α = 0.50 the MAD is 12.33, so α = 0.10 is better. The model with the lowest error wins.

ReferencesClass slides: Heizer & Render, Operations Management (11th ed.), Ch. 4 “Forecasting”. Stevenson, Operations Management, Ch. 3 “Forecasting”: “Approaches to Forecasting”, “Qualitative Forecasts”, “Forecasts Based on Time-Series Data”, “Associative Forecasting Techniques”, “Monitoring Forecast Error”, “Choosing a Forecasting Technique”.

6Managing Quality

  • Definition (ASQ): the totality of features and characteristics of a product or service that bears on its ability to satisfy stated or implied needs. Views: user-based, manufacturing-based (conformance), product-based.
  • Quality raises profit two ways: more sales (response, pricing, reputation) and lower costs (productivity, less rework/scrap, lower warranty).
  • Costs of quality: prevention, appraisal, internal failure, external failure.
  • Six Sigma: 3.4 defects per million opportunities (±6σ; ±3σ is about 2,700 per million). Started at Motorola, used by Honeywell and GE. DMAIC: Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control. Roles: Champions, Black Belts, Green Belts.
  • TQM: a long-term management approach built on customer satisfaction (internal and external customers) with all members participating.

Seven basic tools

Check sheet, histogram, Pareto chart, cause-and-effect (fishbone/Ishikawa) diagram, scatter diagram, control chart, flow chart.

  • Pareto: the 80/20 rule. The “vital few” causes drive most problems, so focus on those over the “trivial many”. Example: missing buttons (217) and stitching errors (112) lead the garment defects.
  • Fishbone: causes grouped by Method, Machine, Man, Material, Measurement, Management, Environment.

Control charts

  • Common (chance) causes: random and unavoidable. Assignable (special) causes: identifiable and correctable.
  • Center line = μ. UCL = μ + kσ and LCL = μ − kσ (k is usually 3). Points should scatter randomly inside the limits.
  • Variables charts: x̄–R, x̄–s, CUSUM, EWMA. Attribute charts: p, np, c, u.
x̄ chart: CL = x̿, UCL/LCL = x̿ ± A₂·R̄
R chart: CL = R̄, UCL = D₄·R̄, LCL = D₃·R̄
Example factors for n = 5: A₂ = 0.58, D₃ = 0, D₄ = 2.11   Sampling: σ(x̄) = σ/√n

Practice: check the R chart first. If variability is out of control, the x̄ chart is not meaningful. The standard set-up is 20–25 samples of 4–6 observations.

ReferencesClass slides: Heizer & Render, Operations Management (11th ed.), Ch. 6 “Managing Quality”. Stevenson, Operations Management, Ch. 9 “Management of Quality” (“Total Quality Management”, “Problem Solving and Process Improvement”, “Quality Tools”) and Ch. 10 “Quality Control” (“Statistical Process Control”, “Process Capability”).

7Supply Chain Management

  • Supply chain: every party involved, directly or indirectly, in fulfilling a customer request: suppliers, manufacturers, distributors, retailers, customers, plus transporters and warehouses. It has flows of products, information and funds. Objective: maximise overall supply chain surplus.
  • Cycle view: procurement, manufacturing, replenishment and customer order cycles between successive stages.
  • Push/pull view: push processes run in anticipation of demand (forecast-based, need safety stock). Pull processes run in response to an actual order. The push–pull boundary is where they meet.
  • Six drivers: facilities, inventory, transportation, information, sourcing, pricing. They set the efficiency vs responsiveness trade-off.
  • Lack of coordination comes from misaligned incentives and information distortion. It raises manufacturing and inventory cost and hurts service.

Bullwhip effect

Order variability grows as you move upstream, away from the end customer. Examples: P&G Pampers (steady diaper demand but swinging factory orders) and Barilla (vendor-managed inventory as the fix).

CauseCountermeasure
Demand forecast updating (each stage forecasts from orders, not true demand)Share point-of-sale data
Order batchingSmaller, more frequent replenishment; third-party consolidation
Price fluctuation (promotions, forward buying)Stabilise pricing

8Organizational Structure

Six building blocks of organization design

Work specialization, departmentalization (by function, product, geography or customer), chain of command, span of control, centralization/decentralization, formalization.

StructureIdeaTrade-off
FunctionalGroup by specialty (marketing, ops, finance, HR)Efficient, but silos
DivisionalGroup by product, region or customerSemi-autonomous divisions
MatrixDual reporting lines (functional + project)Flexible, but complex
FlatFew levels, owner-centredFast, informal
  • Tall vs flat: a tall structure has many levels and a narrow span (tight control, slower decisions). A flat one has few levels and a wide span (faster decisions, more autonomy).
  • Centralised = consistent, top-down control. Decentralised = faster local decisions, builds managers, suits dynamic environments.
  • Coordination: synchronising people and departments toward common goals (Mary Parker Follett: “the glue that holds an organization together”). Types: internal/external × vertical/horizontal. Follett’s principles: direct contact, early stage, reciprocal relations, continuing process. Techniques: committees, liaison roles, integrators, standard rules.
  • Personnel management: obtain, develop and maintain a competent workforce. Functions: manpower planning, recruitment and selection, training, compensation, appraisal, welfare and industrial relations. Employee life cycle: procurement → development → compensation → integration → maintenance → separation.

9Leadership (and Motivation)

Motivation theories

  • Maslow’s hierarchy: physiological → safety → social → esteem → self-actualization. Unmet lower needs motivate first.
  • Herzberg two-factor: hygiene factors (salary, policy, conditions, supervision) prevent dissatisfaction. Motivators (achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, growth) drive satisfaction.
  • McGregor Theory X (people dislike work, need control) vs Theory Y (people seek responsibility and growth).
  • Vroom expectancy: Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence.
  • McClelland: need for achievement (nAch), affiliation (nAff), power (nPow).

Leadership theories

  • Evolution: trait (1930s–40s) → behavioral (1940s–60s) → contingency/situational (1960s–80s) → modern (1980s on).
  • Blake & Mouton Managerial Grid (concern for people vs production): Impoverished (1,1), Country Club (1,9), Authority-Obedience (9,1), Middle-of-the-Road (5,5), Team Management (9,9, the ideal).
  • Hersey & Blanchard situational leadership: telling, selling, participating, delegating, matched to follower readiness.
  • Fiedler contingency model: match the leader’s fixed style (task- or relationship-oriented) to situation favourability: leader–member relations, task structure, position power.
  • Transformational leaders inspire followers toward a larger vision (idealized influence, inspirational motivation). Transactional leaders manage through reward for effort (contingent reward, management by exception).
ReferencesClass slides only (“Motivation Theories”, “Leadership Theories”); no source book is named in the PDF. Background: Robbins, Coulter & Langton, Management, chapters on motivation and leadership.

10Engineering Economics

Comparing mutually exclusive alternatives (MEAs)

  • Choosing one excludes the others. Alternatives must give comparable usefulness. Start from the base alternative (minimum capital that works). Accept extra investment only if the incremental benefit beats the MARR.
  • Investment alternatives have up-front capital and positive cash flows: PW must be > 0 at MARR; choose the largest PW. Cost alternatives are all negative cash flows: choose the largest (least negative) PW.
  • Study period: equal lives are compared directly with PW, FW or AW (all give the same decision). Unequal lives use the repeatability assumption (common multiple of lives) or the co-terminated assumption (a fixed, identical study period).

Example 1: MARR 10%, 5 years. A: −$100k, +$34k/yr. B: −$125k, +$41k/yr. PW(A) ≈ $28.9k and PW(B) ≈ $30.4k (using P/A = 3.7908), so B.
Example 2: MARR 12%, 4 years. C: −$80k, −$25k/yr. D: −$60k, −$30k/yr. PW(C) ≈ −$155.9k and PW(D) ≈ −$151.1k (using P/A = 3.0373), so D.

Breakeven analysis

TC = FC + vQ   R = rQ   Profit = (r − v)Q − FC
Q_BE = FC / (r − v)   (lower v → lower Q_BE)

Example: FC = $75,000/month, r = $8, v = $2.50 → Q_BE = 13,636 units/month. At 15,000 units, profit = $7,500/month.

Two alternatives: write PW/AW/FW of each as a function of a common variable and equate them. Below the breakeven pick the higher-variable-cost option, above it pick the lower. Make/buy example: X = 4,116 units/yr; if production > 4,116, make.

Payback period

Time for inflows to recover the investment. No-return payback (i = 0) ignores time value of money and cash flows after payback. Discounted payback (i > 0) is better for judging risk. Use payback as a screening tool only; decide with PW or AW at the MARR.

ReferencesSullivan, Wicks & Koelling, Engineering Economy (15th ed.), Ch. 6 “Comparison and Selection Among Alternatives”. Class slides: “Breakeven and Payback Analysis” (slides numbered as Ch. 13; the book is not named).

11Entrepreneurship

Ch. 1 Introduction to Entrepreneurship

  • Top reasons to start a firm: be your own boss, pursue your own ideas, financial rewards.
  • Successful entrepreneurs: passion for the business is number one, plus product/customer focus and tenacity. Corporate entrepreneurship (entrepreneurial intensity) is the same idea at firm level.
  • Myths: entrepreneurs are born, not made; they are gamblers (they are moderate risk takers); they must be young (peak age is 35–45).
  • Four-step process: decide to become an entrepreneur → develop successful business ideas → move from idea to firm → manage and grow the firm.

Ch. 2 Recognizing Opportunities and Generating Ideas

  • Opportunity = a favourable set of circumstances creating a need for a new product, service or business. Three ways to find one: observe trends, solve a problem, find gaps in the marketplace.
  • Trends: economic, social, technological, political/regulatory.
  • Personal factors: prior industry experience, social networks (weak ties give more new ideas than strong ties), creativity.
  • Idea techniques: brainstorming, focus groups (5–10 people, trained moderator), library and Internet research.

Ch. 3 Feasibility Analysis

A preliminary check of whether an idea is viable, in four parts: product/service (desirability, demand via buying-intentions survey and “gumshoe” research), industry/target market (attractiveness), organizational (management prowess, resource sufficiency), financial (start-up cash needed, performance of similar businesses, overall attractiveness).

Ch. 4 Writing a Business Plan

Readers are employees and investors. Outline: Executive Summary (most important), Company Description, Industry Analysis, Market Analysis, Marketing Plan, Management Team & Structure, Operations Plan, Product/Service Design & Development Plan, Financial Projections. Types: full plan, summary plan, and a short “deck”/pitch version.

Ch. 5 Industry and Competitor Analysis

  • Porter’s Five Forces: threat of new entrants (barriers: economies of scale, product differentiation, etc.), threat of substitutes, rivalry among existing firms, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers.
  • Industry types: emerging (first-mover advantage), fragmented (consolidation), mature, declining, global.
  • Know competitor types, ethical sources of competitive intelligence and the competitive analysis grid.

Ch. 6 Developing an Effective Business Model

A business model is the plan for how a firm competes, uses resources, structures relationships, interfaces with customers and creates value. Example: Dell’s direct, build-to-order model. Two fatal flaws: a complete misread of the customer, and utterly unsound economics (Pets.com).

ReferencesBarringer & Ireland, Entrepreneurship: Successfully Launching New Ventures, Ch. 1 “Introduction to Entrepreneurship”, Ch. 2 “Recognizing Opportunities and Generating Ideas”, Ch. 3 “Feasibility Analysis”, Ch. 4 “Writing a Business Plan”, Ch. 5 “Industry and Competitor Analysis”, Ch. 6 “Developing an Effective Business Model”. 12Technology Life Cycle

The PDF has no separate technology life-cycle lecture. This topic is built from the Stevenson textbook passages on technology and product life cycles.

  • Key idea: technologies go through life cycles, just as many products do. This affects R&D on two fronts: sustaining growth over the current technology’s life cycle, and planning to take part in the next-generation technology.
  • R&D types: basic research (advance knowledge, no near-term commercial use; usually funded by government and large firms), applied research (aimed at commercial use), development (turns applied results into products). Being first to market can bring higher prices and a temporary monopoly.
  • Short or long cycles: the length of each phase depends on the basic need for the item and the rate of technological change. Some toys and style items last under a year, while washers and dryers last many years before technology replaces them.

Life-cycle stages and strategy (demand over time)

StageFeaturesStrategy
IntroductionTreated as a curiosity; buyers fear bugs and price dropsTrade off getting bugs out vs getting a leap on rivals; time launch (back-to-school, holidays); forecast initial demand so capacity is adequate
GrowthDesign improvements raise reliability and lower cost, demand risesForecast growth rate and duration; make capacity increases match demand
MaturityDemand levels off; few design changes; low costs, high productivityFind new uses to extend life and enlarge the market (baking soda, duct tape)
DeclineDemand fallsDiscontinue and replace, abandon the market, or find new uses or users. Keeping old products is cheap because workers, equipment and supply chain already exist
  • Forecasts of demand and cash flow are key inputs in every phase.
  • Some items (wooden pencils, paper clips, nails, cutlery) show no life cycle. Service life cycles can follow product cycles (installation and repair phase out with old products).
  • Related: process technology (Stevenson Ch. 6) and shorter product life cycles pushing quick-response, flexible manufacturing (Ch. 1, Ch. 7).
