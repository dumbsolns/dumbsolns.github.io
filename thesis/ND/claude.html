The DSM-5-TR groups Neurodevelopmental Disorders as conditions with onset in the developmental period, producing deficits that impair personal, social, academic, or occupational function. They typically manifest early, before school entry, and are marked by a developmental deficit — a skill that is missing or delayed relative to what the brain would ordinarily build at that age — rather than a skill that was acquired and then lost. Each branch below is color-coded like a tab in a patient chart; the color follows that condition through its tags, stat boxes, and the comparison sections at the end.

1 · Intellectual Disability (Intellectual Developmental Disorder)

A disorder of generalized adaptive capacity — not a single missing skill, but a broad ceiling on how independently a person can reason, learn, and function day to day.

Core neuropsychological deficit

A global reduction in general mental ability — reasoning, problem-solving, planning, abstract thinking, judgment, and learning from experience — paired with a matching deficit in adaptive functioning (the practical skills of daily independence). Neither piece is diagnostic alone: a low IQ score without functional impairment, or functional struggle without a cognitive deficit, does not meet criteria.

DSM-5-TR criteria, in plain English

  • A — Intellectual deficits: Confirmed by both clinical assessment and individualized, standardized IQ testing (generally two or more standard deviations below the population mean, roughly IQ ≤ 70, though the number is a guide, not a hard cutoff).
  • B — Adaptive deficits: Failure to meet developmental and sociocultural standards of independence in at least one domain — conceptual (language, reading, money, time, memory), social (empathy, judgment, friendships, social communication), or practical (self-care, job responsibilities, money management, organizing tasks) — such that ongoing support is needed across settings like home, school, work, or community.
  • C — Onset during the developmental period: Deficits begin in childhood or adolescence, not acquired later in life through injury or disease.
Clinical note: DSM-5-TR intentionally de-emphasizes the IQ number and grades severity by adaptive functioning instead — a philosophical shift from prior editions, because two people with an identical IQ score can have very different levels of independence.

Etiology

  • Genetic: Chromosomal conditions (Down syndrome, Fragile X), single-gene disorders, and inborn errors of metabolism (e.g., untreated PKU) account for a large share of moderate-to-profound cases.
  • Prenatal: Maternal infection (e.g., congenital rubella, CMV), fetal alcohol spectrum exposure, teratogen or toxin exposure, and severe maternal malnutrition.
  • Perinatal: Extreme prematurity, birth asphyxia, and neonatal complications such as severe jaundice.
  • Postnatal / environmental: Traumatic brain injury, CNS infection (meningitis, encephalitis), lead exposure, and profound early psychosocial deprivation can contribute, especially at milder severities where etiology is often multifactorial or unidentified.

Evidence-based treatment

  • Early intervention: Infant/toddler developmental programs are the single strongest lever — earlier engagement predicts better long-term adaptive outcomes.
  • Educational: Individualized Education Programs (IEPs), applied behavior analysis for skill-building, and structured, repetition-based instruction.
  • Adaptive/vocational: Life-skills training, supported employment programs, and community-living supports scaled to severity.
  • Pharmacological: No medication treats intellectual disability itself; medication targets co-occurring conditions (ADHD, anxiety, irritability, self-injurious behavior — e.g., risperidone/aripiprazole for severe irritability, particularly when ID co-occurs with ASD).
  • Family-centered care: Caregiver training and respite support materially improve outcomes, since family capacity is itself part of the child's environment.

Critical distinctions

Distinguished from Specific Learning Disorder by breadth: SLD is a circumscribed deficit in one academic domain against an otherwise average IQ, while Intellectual Disability is global. Distinguished from Global Developmental Delay only by age and testability, not by nature. When ASD and low IQ co-occur, both diagnoses are given, and adaptive/social deficits must exceed what IQ alone would predict to justify the ASD diagnosis.

2 · Communication Disorders

A family of four distinct conditions, all involving disrupted use of language or speech in the absence of a broader intellectual or autism-spectrum explanation.

Core neuropsychological deficit

A breakdown somewhere along the pipeline of language: forming ideas into words and grammar (language), articulating sounds clearly (speech), producing fluent uninterrupted speech (fluency), or using language appropriately in social context (pragmatics) — while comprehension of the world and nonverbal reasoning remain intact.

The 4 subtypes — DSM-5-TR criteria in plain English

Severity / specifiers

No formal numeric severity scale applies uniformly across the four subtypes (unlike ID or ASD); severity is instead described descriptively (mild/moderate/severe impact on intelligibility or participation) and documented by standardized language/speech assessment scores.

Key epidemiology

Etiology

  • Genetic: Strong familial clustering in stuttering and language disorder; candidate genes affect speech-motor planning circuits.
  • Prenatal/perinatal: Prematurity, low birth weight, and early otitis media (chronic ear infections disrupting auditory input during critical language-learning windows).
  • Environmental: Reduced early linguistic input is a risk factor for language delay but rarely a sole cause; bilingual exposure is not a cause of disorder and must be distinguished from typical bilingual development.

Evidence-based treatment

  • Speech-language therapy is first-line across all four subtypes — articulation therapy for speech sound disorder, language-stimulation approaches for language disorder, fluency-shaping/stuttering modification for fluency disorder, and social-skills/pragmatic-language groups for social communication disorder.
  • Parent-implemented intervention: Coaching caregivers to embed language-rich strategies into daily routines has strong evidence in preschoolers.
  • School-based supports: IEP/504 accommodations for classroom participation and reduced speaking-pressure situations.
  • Pharmacological: No medications treat communication disorders directly; treatment is entirely therapeutic/behavioral.

Critical distinctions

Social Communication Disorder vs. ASD: This is the single most tested distinction in this category. Both show pragmatic/social language deficits. ASD additionally requires restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities (stereotyped movements, insistence on sameness, fixated interests, or sensory reactivity) — current or by history. If those are absent, and never were present, Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder is the correct diagnosis. Because restricted/repetitive behaviors can be subtle or missed in early childhood, this diagnosis should be made cautiously and often only after ASD has been carefully ruled out over time.

3 · Autism Spectrum Disorder — Expanded Deep Dive

A lifelong neurodevelopmental profile defined by two core domains, graded across three severity levels, and understood today as a spectrum rather than a single fixed presentation.

Core neuropsychological deficit

A difference in how social information and sensory/behavioral information are processed and integrated — atypical social reciprocity and communication, paired with a preference for predictability expressed through repetitive behavior and restricted interests. Underlying this are documented differences in social-cognitive processing (e.g., joint attention, theory of mind), and in neural connectivity patterns affecting how sensory input is filtered and integrated.

DSM-5-TR criteria, in plain English — the 2 domains

  • Domain A — Persistent deficits in social communication and social interaction (all three required):
    • Social-emotional reciprocity: reduced back-and-forth conversation, reduced sharing of interests/emotions, or difficulty initiating/responding to social interaction.
    • Nonverbal communicative behaviors: reduced eye contact, atypical body language, or difficulty understanding/using gestures and facial expression.
    • Developing/maintaining relationships: difficulty adjusting behavior to social context, difficulty with imaginative/shared play, or reduced interest in peers.
  • Domain B — Restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities (at least two of four):
    • Stereotyped or repetitive motor movements, use of objects, or speech (e.g., hand-flapping, lining up toys, echolalia).
    • Insistence on sameness, inflexible adherence to routines, or ritualized verbal/nonverbal behavior patterns.
    • Highly restricted, fixated interests that are abnormal in intensity or focus.
    • Hyper- or hypo-reactivity to sensory input, or unusual interest in sensory aspects of the environment.
  • Other requirements: Symptoms present in the early developmental period (though may not fully manifest until social demands exceed capacity); cause clinically significant impairment; and are not better explained by Intellectual Disability or Global Developmental Delay alone.

Severity levels — 3 levels, rated separately for each domain

LevelSocial communicationRestricted/repetitive behaviors

Sensory profile

Sensory reactivity differences are now a formal diagnostic feature (Domain B). Presentations vary widely and can co-occur in the same individual:

Hyper-reactivity — pain from tags/seams, distress at loud/sudden sound, aversion to certain textures or lighting Hypo-reactivity — high pain tolerance, under-response to temperature, seeking intense input (spinning, crashing) Sensory seeking — visual fascination with spinning objects, smelling/touching items, craving deep pressure

Historical context: Asperger's Syndrome and PDD-NOS

Prior to DSM-5 (2013), autism was split into separate diagnoses under "Pervasive Developmental Disorders": Autistic Disorder, Asperger's Syndrome (autistic social/behavioral features without clinically significant language or cognitive delay), Childhood Disintegrative Disorder, and PDD-NOS (a catch-all for sub-threshold presentations). DSM-5 folded all of these into one dimensional diagnosis — Autism Spectrum Disorder — because research showed the boundaries between them were not reliably distinguishable across clinicians or over time, and severity/support-need was a more clinically useful way to describe the same underlying spectrum. Many adults diagnosed before 2013 still identify with the term "Asperger's," and clinicians should recognize it as a meaningful part of a person's history and identity even though it is no longer a standalone DSM category.

Key epidemiology

1 in 31US 8-year-olds (CDC, most recent surveillance)
~3.8:1Male : female ratio (likely underestimates female prevalence due to under-recognition)
~70%Have at least one co-occurring psychiatric or developmental condition

Etiology

  • Genetic: Highly heritable (twin studies estimate 60–90%); polygenic, with hundreds of implicated genes plus rare high-impact de novo mutations and copy-number variants; strongly associated with conditions like Fragile X and Tuberous Sclerosis.
  • Prenatal: Advanced parental age (especially paternal), certain prenatal medication exposures (e.g., valproate), and maternal immune activation/infection are associated risk factors — correlational, not proven single causes.
  • Perinatal: Extreme prematurity and low birth weight modestly raise risk.
  • Explicitly not causal: Vaccines. This has been extensively studied and repeatedly disproven; it is included here only because it remains clinically important to address directly and compassionately with families.

Evidence-based treatment

  • Early Intensive Behavioral Intervention (EIBI) / Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA): Strongest evidence base, especially when started early; modern ABA emphasizes naturalistic, play-based, and child-led approaches rather than rigid drilling.
  • Developmental/naturalistic models: Early Start Denver Model, DIR/Floortime — blend developmental and behavioral principles for younger children.
  • Speech-language and occupational therapy: Targets communication and sensory-motor/self-regulation goals.
  • Social skills training for older children/adults, often group-based.
  • Pharmacological: No medication treats the core social/communication features. Risperidone and aripiprazole are FDA-approved for associated irritability/aggression; stimulants or alpha-agonists may address co-occurring ADHD symptoms; SSRIs are used cautiously for co-occurring anxiety.

Critical distinctions

Stereotypic Movement Disorder vs. Tics vs. "Stimming": All three involve repetitive movement, but they differ in purpose and voluntariness. Autistic stimming (rocking, hand-flapping) is typically rhythmic, sustained, and self-regulatory — it helps modulate sensory input or emotion, and is usually not distressing to the person doing it. Stereotypic Movement Disorder is diagnosed separately from ASD only when the repetitive motor behavior itself (not accounted for by the ASD diagnosis) interferes with function or causes self-injury. Tics (see Branch 6) are typically sudden, brief, and described as preceded by an urge, and are far more variable moment to moment than stereotypies. See Branch 6 for the full three-way comparison.

4 · Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder — Expanded Deep Dive

A disorder of self-regulation and executive control, not merely "not paying attention" — presenting in three distinct patterns across the lifespan.

Core neuropsychological deficit

A deficit in executive function — the brain's self-management system. This includes response inhibition, working memory, sustained attention, cognitive flexibility, and emotional self-regulation, linked to differences in prefrontal-striatal circuitry and dopamine/norepinephrine signaling. ADHD is best understood not as an inability to pay attention, but as an inconsistent, effort-dependent regulation of attention and behavior.

DSM-5-TR criteria, in plain English

  • A persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that interferes with functioning, with symptoms present before age 12.
  • Inattention (6+ symptoms for children, 5+ for age 17 and older): careless mistakes, difficulty sustaining attention, seeming not to listen, failing to finish tasks, difficulty organizing, avoiding sustained mental effort, losing items, easily distracted, forgetful in daily activities.
  • Hyperactivity-impulsivity (6+ for children, 5+ for age 17+): fidgeting, leaving seat inappropriately, restlessness, difficulty engaging in activities quietly, "on the go"/"driven by a motor," excessive talking, blurting answers, difficulty waiting turn, interrupting others.
  • Several symptoms present in two or more settings (e.g., home and school/work) — a critical cross-situational requirement.
  • Clear evidence symptoms interfere with, or reduce the quality of, social/academic/occupational functioning, and are not better explained by another mental disorder.

The 3 presentations

Predominantly Inattentive Presentation — inattention criteria met, hyperactive-impulsive criteria not met in past 6 months Predominantly Hyperactive-Impulsive Presentation — reverse pattern Combined Presentation — both criteria sets fully met; the most common presentation, especially in childhood

Presentation can shift over a person's life — hyperactivity often visibly declines with age while inattentive and executive-function difficulties tend to persist, which is why many adults present as inattentive even if diagnosed as combined-type children.

Etiology

  • Genetic: Among the most heritable psychiatric conditions (~74–88%); polygenic, involving dopamine transporter/receptor genes among others.
  • Prenatal: Prenatal alcohol or tobacco exposure, and significant prenatal stress, are associated with elevated risk.
  • Perinatal: Very low birth weight and extreme prematurity are established risk factors.
  • Neurobiological: Delayed cortical maturation and altered fronto-striatal dopamine/norepinephrine signaling are consistently observed, though no single brain scan is diagnostic.

Medication overview

Other evidence-based treatment

  • Behavioral parent training is first-line for preschool-age children, teaching contingency management and structured routines.
  • Classroom behavioral interventions (token systems, structured expectations, preferential seating) and formal school accommodations (504 plan/IEP).
  • Cognitive-behavioral therapy for adolescents/adults, targeting organization, time management, and emotional regulation skills directly rather than symptoms alone.
  • Combined approach — medication plus behavioral intervention — consistently outperforms either alone in major trials (e.g., the MTA study).

Critical distinctions

ADHD inattention is distinguished from the inattention sometimes seen in ASD: in ADHD, attention wanders broadly and inconsistently; in ASD, attention can be intensely fixed on a preferred narrow topic (not a deficit of engagement, but of flexibility). ADHD impulsivity is distinguished from pediatric bipolar disorder by chronicity (ADHD is a constant baseline trait; bipolar mood elevation is episodic). See the full ASD vs ADHD table below.

5 · Specific Learning Disorder

A circumscribed academic skill deficit that stands out against an otherwise typical intellectual profile — the "unexpected underachievement" disorder.

Core neuropsychological deficit

A specific breakdown in the cognitive processes underlying one academic domain — most often phonological processing (reading), orthographic/motor planning (writing), or number sense (math) — while general intelligence and access to adequate instruction are both intact. It is fundamentally a disorder of processing, not of effort or intelligence.

DSM-5-TR criteria, in plain English

  • Difficulty learning/using academic skills, with at least one symptom persisting 6+ months despite targeted help: inaccurate/slow/effortful reading, difficulty understanding meaning of what's read, spelling difficulties, difficulties with written expression, difficulty mastering number sense/facts/calculation, or difficulty with mathematical reasoning.
  • Affected skills are substantially and quantifiably below age expectations, confirmed by individually administered standardized tests, and significantly interfere with academic, occupational, or daily performance.
  • Onset during school-age years, though may not fully manifest until academic demands exceed capacity.
  • Not better explained by intellectual disability, uncorrected sensory impairment, other mental/neurological disorders, psychosocial adversity, language proficiency, or inadequate instruction.

Subtypes / specifiers

With impairment in reading (Dyslexia) — word reading accuracy, fluency, decoding, reading comprehension With impairment in written expression (Dysgraphia) — spelling accuracy, grammar/punctuation, clarity/organization of written expression With impairment in mathematics (Dyscalculia) — number sense, memorization of arithmetic facts, accurate/fluent calculation, mathematical reasoning

Dyslexia and dyscalculia are the alternative terms explicitly noted in DSM-5-TR for the reading and math specifiers, used widely in education and research settings.

Severity

Mild — some difficulties in one or two domains; manageable with accommodations Moderate — marked difficulties requiring intensive, specialized teaching and some accommodations to complete activities Severe — severe difficulties affecting several domains; unlikely to learn skills without ongoing intensive individualized specialized teaching

Key epidemiology

5–15%School-age children, across academic domains
~80%Of SLD cases involve reading (dyslexia)
~1.5–3:1Male : female in clinical referral (closer to equal in epidemiological samples)

Etiology

  • Genetic: Strongly heritable (40–70%), often familial — dyslexia in particular shows some of the strongest gene-linkage findings in learning research.
  • Prenatal/perinatal: Prematurity and low birth weight raise risk, likely via effects on white-matter development in language and numeracy networks.
  • Neurobiological: Reading disorder is associated with atypical activation in left-hemisphere temporo-parietal and occipito-temporal reading circuits; math disorder with parietal lobe number-processing regions.

Evidence-based treatment

  • Structured literacy / Orton-Gillingham approaches: Explicit, systematic, multisensory phonics instruction — the gold standard for dyslexia.
  • Direct math instruction: Explicit strategy instruction and concrete-representational-abstract sequencing for dyscalculia.
  • Assistive technology: Text-to-speech, speech-to-text, and calculators as compensatory accommodations rather than replacements for skill instruction.
  • School accommodations: Extended time, alternative formats, and IEP/504 support.
  • Pharmacological: None treat SLD itself; medication is reserved for co-occurring ADHD, which is common and can worsen academic output independent of the SLD.

Critical distinctions

Distinguished from Intellectual Disability by specificity — the deficit is confined to academic skill domains against average-range general cognitive ability. Distinguished from a language disorder in that spoken language may be intact while only the written/academic modality is affected (though the two frequently co-occur, since phonological processing underlies both).

6 · Motor Disorders

Three conditions united by disrupted motor control — clumsiness, repetitive movement, and involuntary tics — each with a distinct clinical signature.

Core neuropsychological deficit

Disruption in motor planning, execution, or inhibition — ranging from poor coordination of purposeful movement (DCD), to self-soothing repetitive motor patterns (Stereotypic Movement Disorder), to involuntary, sudden movements/vocalizations arising from disinhibited cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical motor circuits (tic disorders).

DSM-5-TR criteria, in plain English — 3 conditions

  • Developmental Coordination Disorder (DCD): Motor coordination substantially below age/opportunity expectations (clumsiness, slowness, inaccuracy in tasks like catching, handwriting, or tying shoes); onset in early development; significantly interferes with daily activities/academics; not better explained by intellectual disability, visual impairment, or a neurological condition affecting movement (e.g., cerebral palsy).
  • Stereotypic Movement Disorder: Repetitive, seemingly driven, purposeless motor behavior (hand-flapping, body-rocking, head-banging, self-biting); onset in early developmental period; interferes with social/academic activities or causes self-injury; not attributable to substance effects, another neurological condition, or better explained by another disorder — includes a specifier for "with self-injurious behavior" and severity ratings (mild/moderate/severe based on whether protective measures are needed).
  • Tic Disorders: Sudden, rapid, recurrent, nonrhythmic motor movements or vocalizations. Subdivided by tic type and duration (below).

Etiology

  • Genetic: Tourette's is highly heritable and often familial, implicating basal ganglia–thalamo–cortical circuit genes; DCD shows more modest familial clustering.
  • Prenatal/perinatal: Prematurity and low birth weight are associated with DCD; prenatal exposures (e.g., maternal smoking) are studied as possible tic-disorder risk modifiers.
  • Neurobiological: Tics are linked to dopaminergic dysregulation and reduced cortical inhibition of the basal ganglia motor loop; stereotypic movement disorder is more associated with sensory self-regulation needs, often overlapping with ASD or ID.

Evidence-based treatment

  • DCD: Occupational/physical therapy using task-specific motor training (practicing the actual functional skill, e.g., handwriting or bike riding, rather than generic "sensory" exercises).
  • Stereotypic Movement Disorder: Behavioral approaches — habit reversal training, differential reinforcement, and environmental modification/protective equipment when self-injury is present.
  • Tic disorders: Comprehensive Behavioral Intervention for Tics (CBIT), built on habit-reversal training, is first-line. Pharmacology (alpha-2 agonists like guanfacine/clonidine first, then antipsychotics like aripiprazole or risperidone for more severe/impairing tics) is reserved for tics causing significant distress or physical harm.

7 · Other Specified / Unspecified Neurodevelopmental Disorder

The clinical safety valve — for real, impairing presentations that don't fit neatly into the six named categories above.

Core neuropsychological deficit

By definition, non-specific: some identifiable developmental impairment in cognition, communication, behavior, or motor skill is present, but it does not meet full criteria for any of the six named categories, or there is not yet enough information (e.g., in a very young child, or a first assessment) to make a more specific diagnosis.

DSM-5-TR criteria, in plain English

  • Other Specified Neurodevelopmental Disorder: Used when symptoms cause clinically significant distress/impairment characteristic of a neurodevelopmental disorder, but do not meet full criteria for any specific category, and the clinician documents the specific reason (e.g., "neurodevelopmental disorder associated with prenatal alcohol exposure").
  • Unspecified Neurodevelopmental Disorder: Used the same way, but the clinician chooses not to specify the reason, often because there is insufficient information to make a more definitive diagnosis (e.g., in an emergency setting or a very brief initial evaluation).

Common presentations captured here

Neurodevelopmental effects of prenatal alcohol exposure (sub-threshold for FASD-specific criteria) Sub-threshold, atypical, or mixed presentations spanning ASD/ADHD/SLD features that don't cleanly meet one category Very early childhood cases where severity/domain assessment is not yet reliable

Key epidemiology

No dedicated prevalence figures exist, by definition — this is a residual coding category rather than a single clinical entity, and its use should decrease over time as follow-up assessment clarifies the picture.

Etiology

Etiology mirrors whichever underlying process is present but incompletely characterized — the category exists precisely because a clean etiological or symptomatic pattern hasn't yet emerged, so no single causal story applies.

Evidence-based treatment

  • Treatment targets the specific functional impairments observed (e.g., speech therapy for communication difficulty, OT for motor delay), rather than a single named protocol.
  • Ongoing developmental surveillance and re-assessment are essential — this diagnosis is often provisional, expected to be refined as the child develops and more information becomes available.

Critical distinctions

The key distinction is procedural, not symptomatic: this category should never be used as a permanent substitute for careful differential diagnosis. It is appropriate when full criteria genuinely aren't met (or can't yet be assessed) — not as a default when a clinician hasn't taken the time to rule out the six specific categories above.

Comorbidity — When Branches Overlap

Neurodevelopmental conditions rarely travel alone. Recognizing overlap changes both assessment and treatment planning.

30–50%Of children with ASD also meet criteria for ADHD
~45%Of children with ADHD have a co-occurring Specific Learning Disorder
~20%Of children with Tourette's also meet criteria for ADHD; up to 50%+ show some ADHD symptoms
~30%Of individuals with ASD also meet criteria for Intellectual Disability
~40–60%Of children with a Language Disorder also show reading-related SLD (dyslexia)
~70%Of individuals with ASD meet criteria for at least one additional psychiatric or developmental diagnosis

Because DSM-5 (unlike DSM-IV) explicitly permits an ASD + ADHD dual diagnosis, clinicians should actively screen for both rather than anchoring on whichever presentation is more visible first. Overlapping executive-function and sensory-regulation difficulties across these categories are the biological rule, not the exception — which is exactly why a single unifying framework for treatment planning is useful.

The Toolbox — Tying It All Together

Think of the developing brain as a workshop being built and equipped over childhood. Each neurodevelopmental disorder reflects a different part of that workshop developing on its own timeline.

🧰

Intellectual Disability

The whole toolbox is smaller and simpler than typical — fewer tools, and using each one takes more support, across every job in the workshop.

🗣️

Communication Disorders

The workshop's intercom system — the tool for sending and receiving messages — is glitchy, even though the workers and other tools are fine.

🧭

Autism Spectrum Disorder

The workshop runs on a different operating system — excellent at deep, focused work and pattern-based tasks, but the social interface and sensory dial calibrate differently than most.

⚡

ADHD

The tools are all there and powerful, but the foreman (executive function) has trouble deciding which tool to pick up, when to put it down, and keeping the whole crew on schedule.

📐

Specific Learning Disorder

One specific machine on the floor — the one that processes letters, or numbers — is miswired, while every other machine runs at full, typical capacity.

🎛️

Motor Disorders

The workshop's physical movement controls — the robotic arms — either move imprecisely (DCD), loop on their own (stereotypies), or occasionally fire without being asked (tics).

🗂️

Other/Unspecified NDD

The workshop clearly needs some adjustment, but the blueprint isn't finished yet — this is the folder for "still figuring out exactly which part," not a diagnosis to file away and forget.
